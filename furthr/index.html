Campaign Overview
April 2026 was a month of steady ground-level intelligence gathering. The market showed clear signs of life — a meaningful number of prospects engaged in genuine conversation, and several meetings were secured — but the overall picture was one of a space that is increasingly crowded with incumbent solutions. A large proportion of the conversations reached were substantive enough to reveal real buying context, and the pipeline of callbacks and follow-ups built throughout the month suggests there is latent interest that has not yet converted. The month felt like planting seeds rather than harvesting, with the market broadly aware of the carbon reporting category but not yet compelled to move.

This was the dominant objection of the month, appearing across company sizes and sectors. It signals that the category is well understood but that Furthr is entering conversations after the buying decision has already been made. The focus should shift to uncovering contract end dates and positioning around the limitations of current solutions — particularly around flexibility, cost, and scalability — rather than leading with product features.

Not the right person / handled elsewhere ~7 conversations
"Sustainability reporting and carbon data management are handled by their head office, and he doesn't get involved in those matters."

A significant number of contacts, while reachable, had no decision-making authority over sustainability tools. This suggests the contact data may benefit from a review to better target sustainability leads at business-unit or group level. Where referrals were offered, they should be pursued immediately — and where they were not, switchboard escalation is a viable next step.

Locked into a long-term contract ~3 conversations
"They work with Mondra for their needs and it is a long-term contract, making my offer not relevant at this time."

Contract lock-in is a timing objection rather than a values objection. These prospects should be flagged for re-engagement six to nine months before their contract renewal window. The goal with these contacts is to plant doubt about their current solution's capabilities — particularly around scope three granularity and data volume — so that Furthr is front of mind when they next evaluate.

Platform too expensive or not flexible enough ~2 conversations

Price and configurability concerns indicate that prospects who have previously evaluated Furthr or similar platforms have unresolved questions about ROI and customisation. What Prospects Asked
How did you get my personal number? ~2 conversations

Prospects are increasingly aware of data sourcing practices and are questioning the provenance of their contact details. This reveals a growing sensitivity around privacy in the sustainability space — likely professionals who are active on LinkedIn and receive high volumes of outreach. Transparency about data sourcing, offered proactively and confidently, can convert a defensive moment into a trust-building one.

Can you call back at a specific time? ~8 conversations

The frequency of callback requests suggests genuine but time-pressured prospects rather than outright rejection. Many were mid-meeting or mid-task when reached. This is actually a positive signal — it indicates the contact was not hostile enough to decline outright. Rigorous callback scheduling and follow-through is critical to converting this latent interest into meetings.

Can you send an email instead? ~3 conversations

Several prospects defaulted to email as their preferred channel, which often functions as a soft deflection but can also represent a genuine preference for asynchronous communication. A strong, concise follow-up email sequence — tailored to the specific context of each call — is essential to keep these prospects warm and move them toward a conversation.

Market Themes
Scope three is the pressure point, not scope one or two

Multiple prospects referenced scope one and two reporting as essentially solved problems within their organisations, while scope three — with its supply chain complexity and third-party dependencies — was consistently flagged as the area of ongoing challenge. One prospect explicitly noted their colleagues handle scope three with third-party SAP integration, and another charity contact acknowledged that data management around scope three was expected to become more difficult as volume grows. Furthr's messaging should lean heavily into scope three as the primary pain point.

The Excel-to-platform transition is still happening

A meaningful subset of organisations are still operating with spreadsheet-based approaches, often supplemented by an external associate or consultant. These contacts recognise the limitations but have not yet committed to a platform solution. The window for conversion is open but requires a clear articulation of what the transition looks like in practice — particularly around implementation burden and time-to-value.

Centralised sustainability functions are blocking frontline access

In larger organisations, sustainability reporting is increasingly consolidated at group or head office level, leaving site-level or regional contacts unable to engage meaningfully with new solutions. This structural pattern means that cold outreach to individual sustainability managers at large enterprises is often a dead end unless it is paired with a strategy to access central procurement or group ESG leads directly.

Prospects are fatigued by cold outreach in this category

Several contacts either hung up immediately, cited TPS registration, or expressed frustration at the volume of calls they receive. This suggests the sustainability reporting space is becoming increasingly saturated with vendor outreach, and that a differentiated opening — one that leads with insight or a specific sector reference rather than a generic product introduction — will be necessary to earn time in this market.

Future interest is real but seasonally deferred

A recurring pattern across the month was prospects expressing genuine interest in the category but pointing to autumn or later in the year as their window for evaluation. One contact confirmed they were not looking to expand their current solution until autumn, and several others framed callbacks in terms of months rather than days. This suggests a pipeline-building phase now could yield a strong conversion period in Q3.

Recommended Actions
1

Build a dedicated re-engagement sequence for contract-locked prospects — flag their estimated renewal windows and initiate outreach six to nine months prior, leading with a question about how their current solution handles scope three data volume and granularity.

2

Develop a sector-specific opening for social housing, small charities, and other lower-complexity verticals that acknowledges their scale and frames Furthr's value in terms of efficiency and audit-readiness rather than enterprise-level reporting complexity.

3

Prioritise immediate follow-up on all callback-committed contacts from April — the volume of deferred conversations represents the highest-probability pipeline, and each should receive a personalised confirmation email within 24 hours of the agreed callback time.

4

Review contact data quality to reduce the proportion of calls reaching non-decision-makers — focus on identifying group-level or head-office sustainability leads in larger organisations where frontline contacts consistently deflect to central teams.

5

Create a concise, insight-led follow-up email template for prospects who requested email communication — it should open with a scope three-specific insight or data point rather than a product pitch, to earn attention in an inbox likely to receive multiple vendor approaches.

6

Flag PlanetMark as a priority competitive focus — the prospect currently using PlanetMark expressed specific interest in breaking down emissions by fuel type, which represents a direct feature conversation Furthr should be prepared to have in depth as a differentiator during the scheduled Wednesday follow-up.

Common Objections
Not the right time ~18 conversations
"We're halfway through a project right now — come back to us in Q3 and it might be a different conversation."

This is the most common form of deferral and should not be read as disinterest. It typically signals a prospect who sees the relevance but cannot justify the distraction of a vendor evaluation during a busy period. The opportunity here is to agree a specific follow-up date immediately and treat this as a warm pipeline rather than a lost contact. A lightweight touchpoint — a relevant insight or case reference — in the intervening weeks can keep Furthr front of mind without applying pressure.

We already have something in place ~14 conversations
"We've got a system for that — it's not perfect but it does the job."

This objection masks a common reality: the existing solution is tolerated rather than valued. The phrase 'it does the job' rarely means 'it works brilliantly' — it tends to mean 'we've stopped noticing how much it costs us.' The most effective response is not to challenge the tool directly but to ask what 'doing the job' looks like in practice, which often surfaces the friction points the prospect has normalised. Furthr's positioning should focus on what the current setup cannot do rather than why it should be replaced wholesale.

Budget isn't there right now ~11 conversations
"Honestly, budget is tight across the board — we're not taking on anything new until at least the autumn."

Budget objections in May are worth taking seriously given the broader economic climate, but they are rarely the whole story. In a number of cases this month, the budget objection emerged early — before the prospect had fully understood the proposition — which suggests it was being used as a reflexive deflection rather than a considered position. Where budget is a genuine constraint, the conversation is still valuable: understanding the timeline, the budget cycle, and the internal decision-making process means Furthr can re-enter at exactly the right moment.

Need to involve others before deciding ~9 conversations
"I'd need to loop in my director before we went any further — it's not just my call."

This is a healthy objection in the sense that it confirms the prospect is taking the conversation seriously enough to think about internal process. It also reveals that Furthr is often reaching a middle layer of management who have influence but not final authority. The recommended approach is to treat the initial contact as a champion-building conversation — equipping them with the right framing and materials to take the idea upward — rather than attempting to close before the right stakeholders are involved.

Not sure it applies to us ~7 conversations
"I'm not sure we're quite the right fit for what you're describing — we're a bit different to your typical customer."

This objection often reflects a gap in how the proposition has been contextualised rather than a genuine mismatch. Prospects who say this have usually heard a general description of Furthr and cannot yet see how it maps to their specific situation. The solution is a more sector-specific or role-specific opening that signals immediately that Furthr understands their world. When a prospect feels recognised early in the conversation, the 'not sure it applies' objection rarely surfaces.

Too busy to engage right now ~6 conversations
"This isn't a great time — can you send something over and I'll have a look when things calm down."

The request to 'send something over' is almost always a polite close to the conversation rather than a genuine commitment to review materials. The more productive response is to treat the busyness itself as a signal — ask what is consuming their time right now, and look for the connection between that pressure and what Furthr can address. If the timing genuinely cannot shift, a very specific call-back with a clear agenda is more likely to convert than a follow-up email into a busy inbox.

What Prospects Asked
How does it actually work in practice? ~16 conversations

This is the most encouraging question a prospect can ask — it signals they have cleared the initial scepticism hurdle and are now trying to visualise adoption. It tends to come from people who are genuinely evaluating rather than politely deflecting. The answer should be concrete, step-by-step, and ideally grounded in an example that mirrors their context. Abstract explanations at this stage risk losing momentum.

What does implementation look like and how long does it take? ~12 conversations

Implementation anxiety is a reliable indicator that the prospect is seriously considering the proposition. They are not asking whether Furthr works — they are asking whether they can absorb the change. This is also a question that often conceals a concern about internal resource: who in their team would own this, and how much of their time would it demand? Clear, honest answers about the onboarding process — including realistic timelines and what Furthr takes off their plate — will significantly reduce this barrier.

Can you give me an example of a company like ours that uses it? ~10 conversations

Social proof is being actively sought, which means prospects are not yet at the point of trusting the proposition on its own terms. This question comes most often from prospects who are intrigued but cautious — they want permission from a peer before they feel comfortable moving forward. Sector-specific case references or brief proof points that map closely to the prospect's profile will do more work than general testimonials.

How does pricing work? ~9 conversations

Pricing questions at the cold call stage are a double-edged signal. On one hand, they confirm enough interest to begin mentally committing to a decision process. On the other, if the price is surfaced without sufficient context around value, it becomes a point of resistance rather than a motivator. The priority at this stage should be to defer a detailed pricing conversation to a discovery meeting, where the specific value for that prospect can be established first.

Who else in our sector uses this? ~7 conversations

A slightly more specific version of the social proof question, and a signal that sector credibility matters to this cohort. Prospects asking this question are benchmarking Furthr against what they perceive to be the norms in their industry. Where Furthr has sector-relevant clients or use cases, naming them (where permitted) will accelerate trust. Where it does not, framing the transferability of the proposition clearly is essential.

What makes you different from what we currently use? ~6 conversations

This is a comparison question being asked by prospects who already have a solution in place and are not immediately dissatisfied. It places Furthr in a position where differentiation needs to be earned rather than asserted. The most effective responses avoid direct criticism of the incumbent and instead focus on the specific capabilities or outcomes that Furthr delivers that the current solution does not — ideally anchored to something the prospect has already indicated matters to them.

Market Themes
The status quo is comfortable enough — until it isn't

A dominant thread across May's conversations was the prevalence of prospects who are operating on legacy processes or makeshift solutions that are working just well enough to avoid a crisis. The trigger for change is rarely a proactive decision — it tends to be a moment of failure or a specific pressure point that makes the cost of inaction suddenly visible. Furthr's messaging will land better if it can name and anticipate those trigger moments rather than leading with capability.

Timing pressure is real but often negotiable

Many of the timing-based deflections this month were genuine rather than evasive — prospects are navigating end-of-quarter pressures, internal projects, and leadership changes that make new vendor evaluations feel like an unwelcome addition. However, the same prospects were often willing to commit to a specific future conversation, suggesting the interest is latent rather than absent. Consistent, well-timed follow-up in June and July could convert a significant portion of this deferred pipeline.

Middle management is engaging, but needs enabling

A recurring pattern this month was reaching contacts who were genuinely interested in Furthr but did not hold the authority to progress a decision unilaterally. Rather than treating this as a dead end, it represents an opportunity to build internal advocates who can carry the case upward. Equipping these contacts with a clear, concise summary of the value proposition — something they can share internally without misrepresenting it — would meaningfully improve conversion from conversation to meeting.

Sector credibility is a threshold requirement

Prospects across multiple conversations flagged an implicit expectation that Furthr would understand their specific context before they were willing to engage seriously. Generic framing was met with polite scepticism; conversations that opened with a sector-specific reference point moved more quickly and with less resistance. This suggests that tailoring outreach by vertical — even at the level of the opening sentence — would have a measurable impact on conversion.

The ROI conversation is not yet being triggered early enough

Very few prospects in May moved naturally to asking about outcomes or return on investment during the cold call conversation — instead, questions centred on how the product works and who else uses it. This suggests the value narrative is being received as an interesting product story rather than a business case. Shifting the framing earlier in the conversation to focus on what the problem is costing the prospect — in time, resource, or risk — would accelerate the conversations that are currently stalling at the interest stage.

Recommended Actions
1

Build a structured follow-up sequence for the 'not right now' cohort — assign specific call-back dates agreed during the conversation, and create a lightweight touchpoint (a relevant insight, a brief case reference, or a single pointed question) to be sent between the initial call and the follow-up. This group represents the highest-conversion opportunity in the near-term pipeline.

2

Develop two or three sector-specific opening framings that signal immediately to the prospect that Furthr understands their world. The pattern this month is clear: conversations that opened with generic language met more resistance; those anchored in a recognisable context moved faster. Even a single tailored sentence at the start of the call can shift the dynamic.

3

Create a concise, shareable one-pager designed specifically for the 'I need to loop someone else in' scenario — not a brochure, but a focused document that frames the problem, the solution, and the relevant proof points in language a champion can use when presenting the idea internally without Furthr in the room.

4

Shift the pricing conversation protocol so that cost is consistently deferred to the discovery meeting, with a clear bridge: 'That's exactly what we'd want to cover properly in a conversation — pricing is structured around your specific situation, so a quick call would let us give you a number that actually means something.' This prevents pricing from becoming a cold-call objection before value has been established.

5

Review the targeting criteria for contacts to identify whether there is a pattern in the 'No, Not Me' outcomes — specifically whether these are reaching people too junior to be relevant or too senior to be the right first conversation. A refined entry-point hypothesis, even a small adjustment in seniority or function, could materially improve the referral rate and reduce the proportion of conversations that close without a forward step.

6

Invest in a small bank of sector-relevant proof points or outcome statements that can be deployed in response to the 'can you give me an example of a company like ours' question. These do not need to be full case studies — a single sentence naming a recognisable outcome in a recognisable context is often enough to clear the credibility barrier and keep the conversation alive.
