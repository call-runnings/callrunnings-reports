Campaign Overview
April 2026 was a month defined by a market that is broadly aware of its carbon reporting obligations but largely satisfied — at least for now — with the solutions it already has in place. The overall tone of conversations was polite and professionally engaged, but the dominant response was one of existing commitment: to long-term contracts, in-house tools, incumbent consultants, or group-level mandates that remove individual decision-making power from the contacts being reached. Rather than a market that doesn't see the value of what Furthr offers, this feels like a market that believes it has already solved the problem, even where the evidence suggests otherwise.

Common Objections
Already have a solution in place ~11 conversations
"They work with Mondra for their needs and it is a long-term contract, making my offer not relevant at this time."

This was the single most dominant objection of the month and reflects a market that has moved past the awareness stage. Prospects are not questioning whether carbon reporting requires a solution — they believe they already have one. The strategic opportunity lies in probing what that solution cannot do: fuel-type breakdowns, scalability, flexibility, cost. Furthr's messaging should be calibrated to address the limitations of entrenched solutions rather than leading with the problem itself.

Handled by head office or another team ~5 conversations
"Sustainability reporting and carbon data management are handled by their head office, and he doesn't get involved in those matters."

A structural objection that reflects genuine organisational reality in larger businesses. The contact reached is not the decision-maker and often cannot provide a referral. This signals a need to improve contact targeting at the research stage — specifically identifying whether sustainability decisions are made locally or centrally before the dial is placed, to avoid wasted conversations with non-influencers.

Too expensive / not flexible enough ~2 conversations
"They write their own code and have previously looked into platforms like ours, but found them too expensive and lacking needed flexibility."

A perception objection tied to prior experience rather than current pricing. This is recoverable — particularly where the product has evolved since those earlier evaluations. It points to an opportunity to reintroduce Furthr to prospects who have previously investigated but not converted, with a focus on what has changed and where flexibility has improved.

Not the right time / busy ~8 conversations
"He was about to go into a meeting and requested an email instead."

A high volume of calls landed at genuinely inconvenient moments. While some of these reflect unavoidable timing, the pattern suggests that call scheduling and time-of-day targeting may need refinement. The positive framing is that most of these prospects did not object to the proposition — they simply weren't available. Converting these into firm callbacks is a sequencing priority.

Doesn't handle sustainability / wrong person ~5 conversations
"He mentioned that he does not handle sustainability reporting, which is not his responsibility."

Reflects data accuracy challenges within the prospect list. A portion of dials are reaching contacts whose roles have shifted or were incorrectly categorised. These conversations have limited value unless a referral is obtained. Building a consistent referral ask into these calls — as was done successfully in at least one instance — would convert these otherwise lost dials into intelligence.

Registered with TPS / do not call ~2 conversations
"They don't take cold calls as they are registered with the telephone preference service."

A compliance-related objection that requires immediate list scrubbing. Beyond the legal obligation, these interactions carry reputational risk if not handled promptly and correctly. Records should be updated in real time when TPS registration is flagged.

Prefers email over calls ~3 conversations
"She expressed she's not interested in any selling and prefers emails over calls. She mentioned needing space and time after the call to consider any potential solutions in the future."

A softer objection that nonetheless signals a willingness to remain in conversation via a different channel. These prospects should not be treated as lost — they represent a warm email audience. A structured follow-up email sequence tailored to their specific context (scope of reporting, current tools, scale) would be an appropriate next step.

What Prospects Asked
How did you get my personal number? ~3 conversations

This question came up in multiple calls and reflects growing awareness among professionals about data sourcing and personal privacy. It signals a degree of discomfort with cold outreach to personal devices, and in one case led to a productive redirection to a work email. When this question arises, having a clear, honest and reassuring response — as well as an immediate pivot to professional contact details — is essential both for compliance and for preserving the relationship.

Can you call back at a specific time? ~9 conversations

The high frequency of this request reflects genuine openness to a conversation, just not at that moment. These are among the most valuable outcomes of the month when followed through correctly — a scheduled callback is a soft commitment. It reveals that the proposition is not being rejected outright, but that timing and context matter. Tracking and honouring these precisely is a direct path to increasing meetings from the existing pipeline.

Can you send an email instead? ~4 conversations

Prospects requesting email are often in environments where calls feel disruptive, or they want the opportunity to evaluate quietly before committing to a conversation. This is an evaluation-stage behaviour — they are not dismissing the proposition, they are managing their own process. A strong email follow-up with a clear, specific hook relevant to their context (e.g., scope three data complexity, contract renewal timing) gives this group a genuine reason to re-engage.

Market Themes
The 'Solved It Already' Mindset

The most consistent pattern across April's conversations was the belief among sustainability contacts that their current setup — whether a consultant, a platform, an in-house tool, or a combination — is sufficient. This is less a reflection of genuine capability than of unfamiliarity with the ceiling of those solutions. The conversations that broke through this mindset did so by surfacing specific limitations, such as the inability to disaggregate emissions by fuel type or the coming strain on Excel-based processes as data scales.

Scope Three Complexity is Real but Often Delegated

Several prospects acknowledged managing scope three emissions but noted that it was handled by colleagues, third parties, or head office rather than themselves. This suggests that while scope three complexity is understood as a genuine challenge, organisations are distributing responsibility rather than consolidating it. Furthr's outreach may benefit from mapping this distributed ownership more explicitly, and targeting the individuals who own the most complex or high-volume portions of scope three reporting.

Long-Term Contracts Are Blocking Near-Term Conversion

Multiple prospects referenced being locked into existing contracts — some explicitly multi-year — as the primary reason for disengagement. This is a timing objection as much as a preference one. These contacts represent a high-value future pipeline if re-engagement is timed to contract renewal windows. Capturing indicative renewal dates during these conversations, even informally, would allow for precisely timed re-outreach.

Excel Remains a Widespread but Fragile Baseline

A notable subset of prospects — particularly smaller organisations and charities — are still relying on Excel and associate-level resource for emissions calculations. One prospect explicitly acknowledged that this approach would not scale as data volumes grow, describing it as something 'they are addressing in the background.' This is a strong signal of latent demand among organisations that have not yet formalised their approach, and who are aware of the problem but have not yet acted on it.

Decision Authority is Often Misaligned with Contact Data

A recurring challenge this month was reaching individuals who either no longer hold the role indicated in the data, or who are adjacent to sustainability without owning it directly. In some cases this produced useful referrals; in others the call ended without a path forward. Improving the alignment between contact data and actual decision authority — particularly in larger organisations where sustainability sits across multiple functions — would meaningfully increase the quality of conversations.
