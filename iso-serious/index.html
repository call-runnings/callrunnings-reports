Campaign Overview
May 2026 was a month that revealed the true texture of the ISO compliance market — busy, opinionated, and largely committed elsewhere. The dial volume was strong, and when conversations did happen, they were substantive enough to surface clear patterns about how organisations think about certification, who owns it internally, and what it takes to displace an existing arrangement. The market is not apathetic; if anything, it is engaged — but that engagement is frequently already directed at someone else.

Common Objections
Already have a provider or internal solution ~9 conversations
"They have their own security and compliance team and he is not interested in external partners for managing audit readiness."

This was the dominant objection of the month. It appeared in multiple forms — long-standing external providers, recently signed contracts, and internal teams — but the underlying message was consistent: the need is recognised, but the position is already filled. The opportunity here lies in timing and trigger events. Businesses that have recently acquired other companies, changed structure, or are approaching renewal windows are the most likely to be open. These should be prioritised in future sequencing.

Not the right person / no decision-making authority ~6 conversations
"She informed me that she has no decision-making power and is not the right contact for this matter."

A recurring structural barrier where the call reached someone aware of ISO but not empowered to act on it. In several cases no referral was offered. This points to a contact data challenge — the list may be reaching operational contacts rather than those with procurement or compliance authority. Refining targeting towards roles such as Head of Compliance, Quality Manager, or Operations Director is likely to improve conversation quality.

Not relevant to our business ~5 conversations
"ISO certifications are not relevant to their digital marketing work. He doubts this will change in the future."

Some prospects are genuinely outside the addressable market — digital agencies, businesses that have relocated from the UK, or organisations whose work does not require accreditation. These are clean disqualifications and are useful for tightening the list. However, it is worth examining whether sector filtering at the list-building stage could reduce the volume of these calls and free up dial capacity for higher-probability targets.

Bad timing — on holiday, in a meeting, or too busy ~5 conversations
"It wasn't a good time for him. I will need to follow up later when he's available."

Several calls landed at genuinely inconvenient moments without resulting in a scheduled callback. These are not hard rejections and should not be treated as such. Building a structured follow-up process for timing-based rebuffs — with a specific agreed date and time where possible — would recover value from these touches.

Do not take cold calls / please remove me from the list ~4 conversations
"He does not take cold calls and was busy with another call."

A small but consistent cohort of prospects reacted negatively to the call itself rather than the proposition. One contact requested removal; another was frustrated about calls to a personal number. This reinforces the importance of data hygiene and opt-out management, but does not indicate a broader problem with the campaign approach.

Just signed with a competitor / recently completed a review ~2 conversations
"They've just signed up with another company last week after months of research, so my timing was off."

Two separate prospects had concluded procurement processes within weeks of being called. This is a timing problem as much as a competitive one. Prospects deep in an evaluation rarely reverse course, but they are worth flagging for re-engagement at the point their contract comes up for renewal. Adding these contacts to a long-cycle nurture sequence with a note of their likely renewal window would be a productive use of the intelligence gathered.

What Prospects Asked
Can you send an email with pricing and an overview of how you can help? ~1 conversations

This came from a prospect actively navigating ISO accreditation following a business acquisition — one of the strongest signals of the month. The request for pricing alongside an overview suggests they are in early evaluation rather than passive curiosity. This type of request indicates a prospect who is self-educating and likely comparing options, meaning a well-structured follow-up email with clear value articulation and transparent pricing tiers would carry real weight.

Can you call back on Friday? ~1 conversations

A prospect who was mid-conference call but did not dismiss the conversation — instead proposing a specific alternative time. This is a meaningful buying signal and suggests genuine openness. Prospects who offer a callback time are worth treating as warm leads rather than standard follow-ups.

What is your name and reason for calling? (via receptionist or gatekeeper) ~3 conversations

Several calls were intercepted by gatekeepers or receptionists before reaching the intended contact. This is a normal part of B2B outreach but indicates that a proportion of the target list sits behind organisational switchboards. Having a concise, confident response to the gatekeeper question — one that communicates relevance without triggering a deflection — is worth refining as a consistent part of the call approach.

Market Themes
Business acquisitions are creating live compliance pressure

At least one prospect explicitly flagged that ISO accreditation had become a hot topic internally as a direct result of recent business acquisitions. This is a meaningful trigger — mergers and acquisitions frequently disrupt existing compliance arrangements and force organisations to reassess their certification posture from scratch. Companies that have recently completed acquisitions or are undergoing post-merger integration represent a high-priority segment worth targeting proactively.

The market is committed, not indifferent

The volume of prospects who already hold ISO certifications, have active providers, or have recently completed procurement processes confirms that demand in this space is real and ongoing. The challenge is not persuading organisations that ISO matters — most already know — but reaching them before or during a decision window rather than after it has closed. Intelligence gathered this month about recently signed contracts and incumbent providers can inform when to re-engage these contacts.

Data quality and contact accuracy are limiting conversion

A notable number of calls resulted in the wrong person answering, disconnected numbers, leavers still on the list, or contacts reached on personal phones. Each of these represents a wasted dial and, in some cases, a negative brand impression. Investing in regular list hygiene and more precise role-level targeting would materially improve the ratio of conversations to dials and reduce unnecessary friction.

Timing is the single biggest variable in this market

Two prospects had signed with competitors within the previous week; others were mid-holiday, in meetings, or had just completed internal reviews. The proposition was rarely the issue — the moment of contact was. This suggests that sequencing strategy, persistence across multiple touchpoints, and trigger-based outreach (based on firmographic signals like growth, acquisitions, or new certifications) would increase the likelihood of landing calls at genuinely receptive moments.

Internal ownership of ISO is deeply entrenched in some organisations

Several prospects described mature, well-resourced internal compliance functions — including dedicated security teams and offshore maintenance teams — that left no obvious entry point for an external partner. These organisations are unlikely prospects in the short term, but may become relevant if headcount pressures, restructuring, or new certification requirements stretch their internal capacity. Flagging them for longer-cycle follow-up is more productive than pursuing them actively now.

Recommended Actions
1

Build a trigger-based re-engagement sequence for prospects who cited recently signed contracts — note their likely contract lengths (one prospect mentioned three years) and schedule outreach timed to 60–90 days before probable renewal windows, using the market intelligence gathered this month as context for the conversation.

2

Prioritise companies that have recently completed acquisitions or mergers as a distinct target segment — at least one prospect this month confirmed ISO accreditation had become a live internal issue as a direct result of business acquisitions, and this trigger consistently creates genuine urgency and openness to external support.

3

Review and refresh the contact list to improve role-level accuracy — a significant proportion of May's calls reached people with no decision-making authority, former employees, or incorrect numbers. Targeting compliance, quality, or operations leads directly at the point of list building will increase the ratio of productive conversations to total dials.

4

Develop a structured callback protocol for prospects who cite poor timing rather than disinterest — where a specific day or time is agreed on the call, these should be treated as warm leads and prioritised accordingly. The prospect who asked for a Friday callback is a clear example of an opportunity that deserves deliberate follow-through.

5

Create a concise, well-structured follow-up email template specifically designed for prospects who request written information — the Nick Sibly lead from Solid Solutions is an active opportunity where ISO accreditation is a current priority. The email should include pricing clarity, a brief overview of the process, and a low-friction call to action to move the conversation forward.

Situation
Matt is Co-Founder and COO at FlatPeak. He has been aware of ISO 27001 for some time — this is the second contact, with the first call in October 2025 and a follow-up in May 2026. ISO certification has been on his radar but has not progressed. He has personal prior experience with ISO 27001, describing having 'gone through it enough times' before, which means he is not coming in blind. The company appears to be in a growth phase, fielding increasing questions from customers about certification.
Pain
Matt is experiencing what he described as 'creeping, increasing questions from customers' about ISO certification. There is no acute crisis, but the pressure is building from the customer base rather than from any internal deadline. He is also sceptical about the value of the process itself, having characterised ISO 27001 as '85% box ticking and 10% actual usefulness' — suggesting his pain includes a frustration with the bureaucratic weight of certification, not just the absence of it. He is time-poor and has deprioritised this repeatedly despite acknowledging it needs doing.
Impact
The growing customer enquiries signal that the absence of ISO 27001 is becoming a friction point in commercial conversations — consistent with the SDR's pitch about it being 'a bit of a bottleneck to bring in more revenue.' While Matt did not explicitly confirm lost deals, the pattern of customers asking 'that sort of thing' suggests it is increasingly affecting trust or eligibility in enterprise or regulated client conversations. The strategic risk is that continued delay allows this to compound as FlatPeak moves upmarket.
Critical Event
No hard deadline was mentioned. Matt explicitly said 'there's no deadlines.' However, he did say he would like to get certification done this year (2026), and the meeting has been booked for the last week of June 2026. The SDR flagged that the typical process takes three to six months, which Matt did not push back on — meaning if he wants certification by end of year, the window to start is narrowing. This is a soft urgency lever the AE can use, not a genuine critical event.
Decision
Matt appears to be the primary decision-maker and likely holds sign-off given his Co-Founder and COO title. He referred to 'a couple of things we need to sort out first' without naming other stakeholders, suggesting he is driving this himself. There is no indication of a formal procurement process, committee, or budget cycle. He did mention he has 'a list of a couple of people I want to speak to properly' before deciding, which implies he is comparing providers. No budget figure or approval process was discussed.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by acknowledging the history — Matt knows who you are, you have spoken twice, and he knows this has been on the back burner. Do not treat this as a cold introduction. Start by validating his situation directly: something like, 'You mentioned customers are increasingly asking for this — I wanted to understand what that looks like for you in practice before we talk about anything on our side.' This gets him talking about commercial reality before you pitch anything. The most important thing to probe is what the 'couple of things to sort out first' actually are — these may be internal blockers, competing priorities, or even a co-founder who needs convincing. You need to know whether those blockers have been resolved or whether this meeting is still premature. His '85% box ticking' comment is the framing he will arrive with, so get ahead of it early. Do not be defensive. Instead, use it as an opening: ask him what his previous experience looked like and what he would want to be different this time. This positions ISO Serious as a pragmatic, efficient operator rather than a consultancy trying to inflate scope. Frame the conversation around speed to certification and minimal internal disruption — he wants this done, not drawn out. Use the three-to-six month window the SDR mentioned to create gentle urgency: if he wants this wrapped up in 2026, the conversation is timely. Do not oversell. Matt is experienced, sceptical, and comparing providers. Come in as a peer who is helping him make a practical decision, not a salesperson trying to close him.
