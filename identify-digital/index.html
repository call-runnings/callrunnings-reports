Campaign Overview
May 2026 was a month that revealed a great deal about the market landscape for Identify Digital's proposition. The volume of dials generated a solid number of connected conversations, and while those conversations were largely substantive enough to qualify prospects clearly, the overwhelming pattern was one of an entrenched market — contacts who are already using something, committed to something else, or structurally unable to make a decision even if they wanted to. The month felt less like resistance to the proposition itself and more like resistance to the moment: the timing, the incumbent system, the organisational layer above.

Common Objections
Already using a system ~12 conversations
"We've been using Simpro for 12 years with 120 users — transitioning from it would be chaotic."

This was the dominant objection of the month and spans everything from well-known platforms to unnamed internal tools. The depth of entrenchment varies significantly — some contacts are locked in contractually or by scale, whilst others simply haven't been given a compelling reason to look. The opportunity here lies in identifying which contacts are satisfied versus which are merely habituated. Jake's case, for instance, shows that an existing system and genuine pain can coexist.

Not the right person / decision goes elsewhere ~5 conversations
"All IT-related decisions go through the CFO."

A recurring structural blocker — contacts either don't hold budget authority or actively deflect to another department. In one case the parent company controlled all software decisions. This suggests the contact lists may be reaching operational rather than strategic decision-makers. Identifying CFO, IT Director, or Operations Director-level contacts within the same organisations could unlock conversations that the current contacts are willing to facilitate.

Not relevant to our business ~6 conversations
"Our site visits are mostly escorted and we follow the site owner's procedures — we don't face issues with job management or risk assessments."

Several contacts felt the proposition simply didn't map to how their business operates — either because they don't perform on-site work, don't manage timesheets directly, or rely on their supply chain for document management. This points to a targeting refinement opportunity: filtering for businesses with field-based workforces and direct responsibility for compliance and scheduling before dialling.

Too busy / bad timing ~5 conversations
"He was busy wrapping up for the day — suggested calling back early afternoon."

A proportion of calls ended due to poor timing rather than genuine disinterest. Several contacts requested callbacks at specific times or agreed to receive emails, which represents a warmer response than a hard no. These contacts should be prioritised for structured re-engagement rather than being recycled into the general dial pool.

Committed for the next 12 months ~2 conversations
"They plan to stick with their current solution for at least the next 12 months."

A time-based objection that is not a permanent no. These contacts have effectively given a future re-engagement date. Logging these with a specific follow-up trigger — rather than a vague future callback — would ensure they are contacted when contract review cycles are more likely to be open.

Doesn't accept cold calls / prefers email ~3 conversations
"He stated they don't accept cold calls and are too busy. He requested an email instead."

A small but consistent group of contacts redirected to email rather than engaging on the call. This is worth treating as a warm signal rather than a rejection — these individuals are not categorically uninterested, they are simply controlling the channel. A well-crafted follow-up email that references the specific pain points mentioned in the call could reopen the conversation.

What Prospects Asked
Can you send more information / a demo link by email? ~4 conversations

Multiple contacts — including Jake, Daniel, Tom, and Brian's referral route — requested email follow-ups rather than continuing the conversation live. This signals a degree of interest that didn't convert to a booked meeting, but represents a pipeline of warm email contacts. The quality of the follow-up email sent in these cases is critical to whether that interest progresses.

What does the platform actually do for procurement and compliance? ~1 conversations

Jake raised procurement management and compliance as genuine pain points even whilst explaining why he wasn't switching from eWorks. This is one of the clearest buying signals in the month's transcripts — a prospect who can articulate specific problems and is actively seeking solutions, even if the timing isn't right. This specific capability deserves more prominent positioning in opening conversations.

Are you able to call back at a better time? ~4 conversations

Several contacts didn't object to the proposition but asked for a different time — early afternoon was mentioned specifically. This is a meaningful signal that the proposition wasn't dismissed, and that call scheduling and timing discipline could meaningfully increase the conversion rate of connected calls into real conversations.

Market Themes
The Market is Well-Served but Not Fully Satisfied

Almost every contact who engaged substantively already had a system in place — yet Jake's conversation revealed that having a system and having a problem are not mutually exclusive. Contacts using eWorks, in-house tools, or off-the-shelf software still cited compliance gaps, procurement challenges, and limitations that they were working around. The opportunity is not to displace satisfaction but to surface the gaps beneath it.

Structural Decision-Making Blocks Are Common

A notable pattern this month was contacts who were personally receptive but structurally unable to act — purchasing decisions gated by CFOs, IT departments, parent companies, or group-wide migrations to platforms like Oracle. These are not soft rejections; they are genuine organisational realities. Identifying the correct decision-maker tier — and in some cases asking current contacts to facilitate an introduction upward — could unlock a layer of the market that is currently inaccessible at first contact.

Timing and Re-engagement Represent a Meaningful Pipeline

A recurring theme was not permanent disinterest but misaligned timing — contacts committed for 12 months, mid-implementation, heading into meetings, or building their own systems in the interim. Tom suggested revisiting in six months; Carl gave a 12-month window; Jake requested a demo link for future use. If these contacts are tracked and re-engaged at the right moment, they represent a pipeline that has already been partially qualified this month.

List Quality Is Surfacing Mismatched Contacts

Several calls this month reached contacts who were categorically outside the target profile — a recruitment professional who shouldn't have been on the list, a prospect at Hampshire County Council reached in error, a contact whose business operates entirely escorted on third-party sites, and a company that doesn't operate on-site at all. Each of these calls represents a dial that couldn't have converted regardless of the conversation. Tightening the target criteria around field-based, directly-responsible-for-compliance businesses would increase the proportion of genuinely qualified conversations.

Recommended Actions
1

Build a structured re-engagement sequence for the contacts who requested email follow-ups — Jake (eWorks user with compliance pain), Tom (revisiting in ~six months), Carl (12-month contract window), and Brian's MD referral at Fenner Group (BShannon@FennerGroup.com) — with tailored messaging that references the specific pain points each raised rather than a generic outreach.

2

Refine the contact list criteria to prioritise field-based businesses with direct operational responsibility for compliance, job management, and timesheets — filtering out businesses that rely on supply chains for document management, operate escorted-only on third-party sites, or are non-contracting office-based operations, to increase the proportion of genuinely qualifying conversations per dial session.

3

Develop a secondary outreach strategy for contacts where the decision-making authority sits above the person reached — asking current contacts to facilitate introductions to their CFO, IT Director, or Operations Director where they've confirmed the proposition is relevant but the decision isn't theirs to make, and targeting those senior roles directly within the same organisations.

4

Introduce call-back scheduling discipline for contacts who requested a specific time or indicated better availability — such as the contact who suggested early afternoon — to ensure that timing-based deflections are treated as warm callbacks rather than returned to the general pool, improving the conversion rate of already-connected numbers.

5

Incorporate procurement management and compliance as primary pain-point hooks in the opening conversation, given that Jake's transcript demonstrated these are live, named challenges even among contacts currently using a competitor platform — leading with these specific problems rather than general job management and timesheets may open more substantive conversations earlier in the call.
