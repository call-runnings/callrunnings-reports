Campaign Overview
April 2026 was a month of genuine market contact — the field service sector is clearly active, opinionated, and mid-movement on technology decisions. A meaningful portion of conversations revealed prospects who are either freshly committed to a new system, deep in implementation, or cautiously watching a home-built solution take shape. The market is not dormant; it is in transition, and that transition is creating both short-term resistance and identifiable future opportunity. The calls that landed best were the ones that found prospects who recognised the pain — manual processes, ageing or expensive platforms, and the operational drag of paper-based workflows — even when the timing wasn't right to act.

Common Objections
Already committed to a recently implemented system ~6 conversations
"He conducted extensive due diligence over 12 months before choosing Simpro, so no immediate opportunities for us."

This was the single most common reason for a firm 'no' in April. Prospects like Paul, James (Paper Drop), Matthew (PinPro), and others had made recent, considered technology decisions and were not willing to revisit them. These are not lost causes permanently — they are prospects who will become re-evaluators when their current system disappoints or their contract ends. The priority is to log contract timelines where known and re-enter the conversation at the right moment.

We handle it in-house ~5 conversations
"They have an in-house IT team that has already automated their tasks with their own system."

A significant cluster of prospects cited internal development — bespoke ERPs, proprietary apps, or dedicated software developers — as their reason for disengaging. This objection often signals a business that has invested heavily in a solution and has organisational pride attached to it. However, prospects like Andy, who admitted their app was 'still being assessed', and Ben, who acknowledged their platform 'has a long way to go', suggest the in-house objection can mask underlying uncertainty. Probing questions about reliability, maintenance cost, and scalability may open these conversations back up.

Not the right time — too much going on ~4 conversations
"They are currently focused on implementing AI and moving offices, making it hard to fit in new projects."

Several prospects acknowledged the relevance of the proposition but cited competing priorities — office moves, AI implementation projects, trade shows, or simply being mid-transition. Ian's response was particularly telling: he expressed genuine frustration with a previous technology investment that underdelivered, which suggests he is a future candidate once his current pressures ease. These prospects warrant a structured follow-up cadence rather than a hard close attempt.

Happy with current setup / not broke, won't fix ~5 conversations
"He has everything covered with different programs for managing jobs and is happy with their current setup."

A number of prospects — James (field service), Jason, Steve, Peter — gave variants of 'we're fine as we are.' This is the hardest objection to move because it reflects a lack of perceived pain rather than a timing or budget issue. The implication for messaging is that the opening hook needs to introduce tension earlier — surfacing the cost of manual processes or the risk of incumbent systems becoming obsolete — before the prospect self-categorises as 'covered'.

No decision-making authority / wrong contact ~4 conversations
"Tom informed me that I reached his personal mobile rather than a work number. He asked how I obtained his contact information."

A portion of calls reached individuals who were retired, had sold their business, were no longer in a relevant role, or were contacted on personal numbers. This points to a data quality issue worth reviewing with the data provider. Tom specifically asked how his details were obtained — a reputational consideration worth flagging for data hygiene purposes.

What Prospects Asked
How did you get my contact details? ~1 conversations

Tom's direct question about data sourcing — and the fact that he was reached on a personal mobile — highlights the importance of data accuracy and the sensitivity prospects have around personal contact information. When this question arises, a clear and confident answer about the data provider (as given referencing Surf) is essential. It also signals that data quality checks should be a recurring part of campaign hygiene.

Can you send me an email with information instead? ~2 conversations

Both Jen and Liam redirected the conversation towards email rather than committing to a meeting or call. This is a common deflection, but Liam's response was more substantive — he acknowledged the relevance of the topic and gave a specific email address, suggesting genuine interest at a low-commitment level. For these prospects, a well-constructed follow-up email that mirrors the pain points discussed in the call is the most productive next step, with a clear and soft call to action.

When should I call back? / Can you call back later? ~5 conversations

A recurring pattern across the month was prospects asking for a callback at a better time — Gary (call back in the morning), Steve (call back in an hour), Simon (try again later), Nigel (on holiday, call back). While these are not warm leads, they do represent contacts who did not reject the proposition outright. The conversion potential here depends entirely on whether the follow-up actually happens and whether the second call catches the prospect in a more receptive moment.

Market Themes
The market is mid-cycle, not static

A striking number of prospects in April were in the middle of something — implementing a new system, building an app, merging with another company, or preparing to move offices. This is not a dormant market; it is a market in active transition. For Identify Digital, this means the pipeline is not primarily about prospects who will never buy, but about prospects who are not ready yet — and timing re-entry correctly will be critical.

Incumbent dissatisfaction is present but quiet

Stuart's situation with Job Watch — expensive, declining in effectiveness, contract ending in December — is likely not unique. Several other prospects mentioned platforms that were described as difficult (PinPro), not fully mastered (Ben's in-house tool), or compromised by other priorities (Ian's AI project complications). The market contains a meaningful cohort of users who are tolerating their current solution rather than championing it, and these prospects are worth identifying and revisiting on a structured timeline.

In-house development is a growing objection category

Five separate prospects cited internal development as their reason for not engaging — ranging from fully bespoke ERPs to near-complete proprietary apps to dedicated internal software teams. This suggests a segment of the field service market that is investing in building rather than buying. However, prospects like Andy and Ben revealed cracks in this confidence, with Andy suggesting a six-month review and Ben admitting their platform had 'a long way to go'. The in-house objection deserves a specific counter-narrative around the long-term cost and risk of maintaining custom solutions.

Referral potential is largely untapped

Harrison at Maine Electrical mentioned his brother works at a related company, Severn Trent. Jason — though personally out of the workforce — provided a referral to Mike, the MD at Greens. These are early signals that the network around engaged prospects can yield secondary contacts. A light-touch referral ask, embedded naturally into discovery conversations, could extend the reach of each productive call without additional dials.

Data quality is affecting reach at the margins

A small but notable portion of calls reached individuals who were retired, had sold or closed their business, or were contacted on personal rather than work numbers. Andrew was closing his business within two months; Lee was retired and frustrated by sales calls; Tom was reached on a personal mobile. While these represent a small fraction of dials, they represent wasted effort and, in Tom's case, a potential reputational touchpoint. A periodic data audit would reduce this friction.
