Campaign Overview — April 2026

April 2026 represented a solid foundation month for Identify Digital's outbound campaign. Across 501 total dials, we achieved 86 connected calls and 62 meaningful conversations — a connection rate of 17.2% and a conversation rate of 12.4% against total dials. From those conversations, 3 meetings were booked and 1 prospect was fully activated, demonstrating early-stage pipeline generation in what remains a considered, relationship-led market.

The full disposition breakdown for the month is as follows:

• Total Dials: 501
• Connected Calls: 86
• Conversations: 62
• Meetings Booked: 3
• Activated: 1
• No, Not Now: 4
• No, Not Ever: 48
• No, Not Me (Referred): 1
• No, Not Me (No Referral): 2

The volume of 'No, Not Ever' dispositions (48) is the most significant figure to contextualise. In a market where bespoke and embedded software solutions are commonplace, a high proportion of firm rejections is expected at this stage of a campaign — many prospects are mid-implementation, locked into existing contracts, or operating with in-house development capability. The 'No, Not Now' category (4) and the referral generated (1) represent meaningful signals worthy of structured follow-up. Overall, April has delivered useful market intelligence, a live pipeline, and a clear picture of where the addressable opportunity sits.

Competitor & Market Intelligence — What the Market Is Using

A notable volume of prospects across April's conversations referenced existing software solutions, giving us a clear view of the competitive landscape Identify Digital is operating within. The following platforms were mentioned directly by name:

**Simpro** — Referenced by one prospect who had spent 12 months conducting due diligence before selecting it. He described the process as thorough and deliberate, suggesting Simpro is winning deals at the enterprise-consideration level. The sentiment was one of commitment and investment rather than enthusiasm.

**HubSpot** — Mentioned by one prospect as the tool being used for booking and timesheet management. He expressed confidence in his current setup, describing his business as 'ahead of the game' with IT systems. This represents an unconventional use of HubSpot and may indicate a gap that a purpose-built solution could address.

**PinPro** — Named by a prospect whose company had recently merged and adopted this platform for field management. He acknowledged the platform was 'challenging' but described the business as committed to it for now, suggesting latent dissatisfaction.

**Job Watch** — One of the more commercially valuable intelligence points of the month. A prospect described Job Watch as 'becoming expensive and less effective,' and confirmed he would likely begin evaluating alternatives from June or July, with a contract end date in December. This is a warm future opportunity.

**Paper Drop** — Referenced by one prospect as a recently implemented solution for job booking and timesheets. Sentiment was neutral and the implementation was recent, making re-engagement unlikely in the short term.

**NetSuite (ERP)** — One prospect mentioned an in-house ERP built on NetSuite, with a dedicated internal resource working on it for over a year. This reflects a growing trend of businesses investing significant internal resource into customising off-the-shelf ERP platforms rather than procuring purpose-built field service tools.

**In-house / bespoke development** — Several prospects referenced entirely self-built platforms, including one actively trialling an in-house developed job management platform, another whose company had a fully bespoke ERP, and a third whose internal IT team had automated processes entirely. One prospect explicitly mentioned having their own software developer on staff. This pattern of internal development is a consistent and recurring competitive force in this market.

"He conducted extensive due diligence over 12 months before choosing Simpro"
"They found it challenging but are committed to it for now — on PinPro"

Common Objections — What the Market Is Saying

The objections encountered throughout April were consistent and revealing. Rather than reflecting a resistance to the value proposition itself, the majority point to timing and incumbency challenges rather than a fundamental mismatch between product and market need.

**1. 'We already have something in place'**
The most frequent objection of the month. Prospects cited a wide range of existing solutions — from enterprise platforms like Simpro and NetSuite to bespoke internal builds. The sheer variety of tools being used suggests the market is fragmented, with no single dominant solution, which represents an ongoing opportunity. Many of these systems appear to have been adopted out of necessity rather than strategic choice.

**2. 'We're currently mid-implementation'**
Several prospects were in active implementation phases and were unwilling to consider alternatives mid-process. This is a timing objection rather than a permanent 'no.' These prospects represent future pipeline if re-engaged at the right moment.

**3. 'We handle it in-house'**
A recurring theme across multiple calls was the presence of internal IT resource — developers, ERP specialists, or dedicated technical staff — who had built or were building bespoke solutions. This reflects a market segment that understands the problem intimately but has chosen self-sufficiency. The key question for these prospects is sustainability and ongoing maintenance cost.

**4. 'It's not relevant to how we operate'**
Some prospects indicated that their business model — for example, working entirely as a subcontractor under larger firms' systems — meant that the offering simply did not apply to them. This is a useful data point for refining targeting criteria going forward.

**5. 'Now isn't the right time'**
Beyond implementation, other timing barriers included office moves, AI integration projects, and general business change. One prospect referenced a previous poor experience with an app investment that had not delivered on its promises, which contributed to his reluctance to engage. This suggests that trust and proof of delivery will be important conversion factors in this market.
"We're currently focused on implementing AI and moving offices, making it hard to fit in new projects"
"They work under larger firms' systems and adapt to their requirements"
"A previous app investment that didn't perform as promised"
"We have only two ASMs and handle contractors adequately — we're covered"

Prospect Questions — What Prospects Wanted to Know

Where prospects did engage in dialogue, their questions and comments revealed a consistent set of concerns. These are worth understanding as they indicate what messaging and materials need to address in order to move prospects further through the funnel.

**1. 'Where did you get my details?'**
At least one prospect directly questioned how his contact information had been obtained, referencing that the call reached his personal mobile. This signals a sensitivity around data sourcing that is present in the market and should be anticipated with a clear, confident response regarding data compliance and sourcing methodology.

**2. 'Can you send me something to look at first?'**
Multiple prospects, rather than agreeing to a meeting, requested email literature before committing to further conversation. This was the preferred lower-commitment entry point for several engaged prospects. It suggests that some decision-makers in this market want to self-educate before engaging in a live dialogue.

**3. Implicit question: 'How is this different from what I already have?'**
Prospects who were using existing systems — particularly those who described their current setup positively — were implicitly asking for differentiation. The challenge of a bespoke or custom platform needs to be communicated in contrast to the limitations of off-the-shelf or internally built alternatives.
"He asked how I obtained his contact information"
"She requested an email with information instead of a meeting right now"
"He will reach out once he has looked over the material"

Market Themes — Broader Patterns from the Field

Across the full set of April conversations, several macro-level themes emerged that paint a detailed picture of the field service software market as it stands today.

**Theme 1: The market is saturated with partial solutions**
Prospects are not operating without systems — they are operating with systems that partially work. From HubSpot being used for timesheets, to bespoke ERPs built over years of internal effort, to platforms described as 'challenging' but persisted with — the pattern is one of tolerated imperfection rather than genuine satisfaction. This is a commercially important distinction: the opportunity is not to replace nothing, but to replace something that is not truly fit for purpose.

**Theme 2: In-house development is a growing competitive force**
A meaningful proportion of prospects had invested — or were actively investing — in building their own solutions. This reflects both the availability of development resource and the complexity of their operational needs. However, bespoke internal builds carry long-term risks around maintenance, scalability, and staff dependency that represent a compelling angle for re-engagement at the right moment.

**Theme 3: Decision fatigue and prior investment create inertia**
Several prospects referenced prior purchasing decisions — sometimes made after extensive due diligence — and were unwilling to revisit those decisions even where the current solution was imperfect. The investment of time, money, and organisational change into an existing platform creates a psychological and financial barrier to switching. Messaging that acknowledges this cost of change and articulates a low-friction transition path may help overcome this inertia.

**Theme 4: Timing is a genuine and recurrent barrier**
The number of prospects who were mid-implementation, mid-office-move, mid-AI-project, or simply 'heading into a meeting' reflects a busy, operationally stretched market. Many of the 'not now' responses were genuinely situational rather than dismissive. A structured re-engagement cadence timed to contract renewals, implementation completions, and seasonal lulls could convert several of these contacts into future pipeline.

**Theme 5: Trust in technology providers has been damaged by past experience**
At least one prospect explicitly referenced a previous app investment that had not delivered on its promises. This erosion of trust is likely more widespread than explicitly stated and represents an important backdrop to every conversation. Proof points, case studies, and transparent communication about implementation timelines will be important in building credibility with this audience.
"Frustrations about a previous app investment that didn't perform as promised"
"Their platform has a long way to go before it's fully mastered"
"Job Watch has been becoming expensive and less effective"
"We're nearing completion of our own app but are still assessing its effectiveness"

Recommended Actions — April 2026

Based on the intelligence gathered throughout April, the following actions are recommended to strengthen pipeline, improve conversion, and ensure the campaign is positioned to capitalise on the market patterns identified.

**1. Prioritise and formalise the Job Watch re-engagement opportunity**
The prospect currently using Job Watch — who described it as 'becoming expensive and less effective' and confirmed a likely evaluation window opening in June or July — represents one of the highest-value leads of the month. A structured follow-up should be scheduled for early June, and in the interim, tailored content addressing the specific limitations of legacy job management platforms should be sent. Contract end date: December. This prospect should be treated as a priority warm lead.

**2. Build a re-engagement sequence for mid-implementation prospects**
Several prospects declined engagement solely because they are mid-implementation of a recently adopted solution. These contacts should not be permanently disqualified. A lightweight re-engagement cadence — scheduled 6 to 9 months from the point of first contact — would allow outreach at a time when early-stage implementation frustrations are likely to have surfaced. One prospect explicitly invited a follow-up in six months.

**3. Develop email nurture content for the 'send me something first' segment**
A recurring prospect behaviour was the request for email literature before committing to a meeting. Rather than treating this as a soft rejection, it should be supported with a short, well-structured email sequence that speaks directly to common pain points — manual timesheet processes, job booking inefficiencies, and risk assessment management. This content should be ready to deploy immediately following any call where a prospect requests information, and should include a clear and low-friction call to action.

**4. Refine targeting to reduce calls to structurally misaligned prospects**
A proportion of conversations this month revealed structural mismatches — prospects working entirely as subcontractors under client systems, prospects who had retired or sold their businesses, and contacts who had no involvement in relevant decision-making. Reviewing and tightening the target criteria — particularly around company type, ownership status, and decision-maker seniority — would improve the quality of conversations and reduce wasted dials.

**5. Introduce social proof and case study material into the opening narrative**
Given the evidence of damaged trust from prior technology investments, and the prevalence of prospects who are committed to hard-won existing solutions, the conversational approach would benefit from leading with credibility signals earlier in the call. References to similar businesses that have successfully transitioned — with specific, quantified outcomes — would help address the scepticism that appears to be present across a significant portion of this market. This is particularly important when speaking to prospects who have previously been let down by software providers.
