Campaign Overview
May 2026 was a month defined by market saturation in the security questionnaire automation space. A meaningful portion of the market has already moved — prospects across the board referenced existing tools, AI-based workarounds, and internally built solutions, reflecting a landscape where the pain point Securilix solves is widely recognised but increasingly being addressed through alternative means. That said, the conversations that did land demonstrated genuine relevance: where prospects were still navigating the problem manually or had solutions they were less than fully confident in, there was clear openness to dialogue.

Common Objections
Already have a tool or solution in place ~5 conversations
"They already have a tool in place that handles most of the heavy lifting, so our solution isn't relevant for them currently."

This was the most prevalent objection of the month and reflects genuine market movement toward automation. However, 'having a tool' does not mean the tool is working well — prospects using AI patchwork or legacy software may still have coverage gaps around accuracy, turnaround time, or audit trails. The response to this objection should shift from feature comparison to diagnostic questioning: what does their current process actually look like under pressure, and where does it break down?

We don't receive enough questionnaires to justify it ~4 conversations
"They receive security questionnaires infrequently (2-6 times every few months). They have found a workaround, indicating it's not a significant issue for them."

Low volume is a legitimate disqualifier for some prospects, but it can also mask a misunderstanding of how much time each questionnaire actually consumes. Prospects in this camp are worth probing on effort-per-questionnaire rather than accepting volume as the full picture. If a small number of questionnaires are still taking hours to complete, the ROI case can still be made.

Not the right person / not my area ~5 conversations
"Yanis indicated that the security questionnaires aren't his area. He suggested I reach out to either Andy Hunter (CSO) or Stuart Hall (main DRC engineer)."

A recurring theme where the person reached sits adjacent to the problem rather than owning it. This is partly a targeting issue and partly an opportunity — when prospects like Yanis name specific stakeholders, those referrals should be actioned immediately and tracked. The positive signal here is that the problem is known within the organisation; the contact simply isn't the decision-maker.

Not relevant — we don't sell to businesses ~2 conversations
"They don't receive security questionnaires as they don't sell to businesses, so my solution isn't relevant."

A hard disqualifier that points to a data quality issue in the contact list. Securilix's value proposition is fundamentally tied to B2B sales cycles — any prospect that sells exclusively to consumers or the public sector without enterprise procurement will not encounter the problem. List segmentation should filter for B2B SaaS, technology vendors, and professional services firms as a baseline.

Bad timing / too busy right now ~4 conversations
"He is currently focused on more pressing tasks. He suggested I send information via email and follow up in Q1 next year."

Timing objections in this campaign often came with soft doors left open — a request to follow up later, a LinkedIn suggestion, or a callback agreement. These should not be treated as rejections. A structured follow-up sequence with a defined re-contact date is essential to prevent these warm leads from going cold. Q1 mentions in particular should be flagged for October/November re-engagement.

We've built our own solution internally ~2 conversations
"He's working on a home-baked solution for handling security questionnaires, indicating potential competition."

Internal builds are expensive to maintain and rarely scale well as questionnaire complexity increases. This objection presents a future opportunity more than a permanent barrier — the prospect is clearly invested in solving the problem, and when their in-house solution begins to show strain, Securilix should already be in the relationship. Staying in contact with a light-touch educational approach is the right play here.

What Prospects Asked
Can you send information over email? ~3 conversations

This request typically signals polite deflection rather than genuine interest, but it also indicates the prospect is not ready to close the door entirely. It reveals that the value proposition hasn't yet been articulated compellingly enough to hold attention in the moment. Email follow-ups in these cases should be short, highly specific to the prospect's context, and end with a clear and low-friction call to action rather than a generic brochure drop.

What do you actually do / what is this about? ~2 conversations

The presence of this question suggests that the opening pitch is not consistently landing with immediate clarity. Prospects who ask this are engaged enough to query further rather than hang up, which is a positive sign — but it indicates the hook needs to be sharper and more immediately relevant to their world from the very first sentence.

Market Themes
The 'Already Sorted' Market: AI and Automation Are Reshaping Buying Intent

The most dominant pattern across May's conversations was prospects positioning themselves as having moved on from the problem — using AI tools, Vanta integrations, trust centres, or internal builds to handle questionnaires. This signals a market that is maturing rapidly, where the pain is well understood but the perceived need for a dedicated third-party solution is under pressure. The implication for Securilix is that the opening conversation must now challenge the adequacy of existing solutions, not simply establish that the problem exists.

Questionnaire Volume Is Not the Right Qualifying Metric

Several prospects dismissed relevance on the basis of receiving questionnaires infrequently, yet others with similar volumes were actively building or purchasing solutions. This suggests that volume alone is a poor proxy for pain — the actual qualifier is the effort, risk, and delay caused by each questionnaire, regardless of how many arrive. Reframing qualification questions around cycle length, deal impact, and team capacity rather than raw volume would likely surface better-fit prospects.

Referral Intelligence Is Being Left on the Table

A handful of calls resulted in named referrals to more relevant stakeholders — CSOs, security engineers, and compliance leads — yet these moments appear to be closing without structured follow-through. In a month where direct connections were difficult to secure, referrals represent a high-value shortcut to the right room. Building a consistent referral-capture habit into every disqualification call could meaningfully improve pipeline quality in the months ahead.

Gatekeeper and Connectivity Friction Is Compressing Conversation Volume

A large proportion of dials in May did not result in substantive conversations due to gatekeepers, automated systems, wrong contacts, and unavailable prospects. This is partly a numbers reality, but it also points to the value of multi-channel sequencing — LinkedIn outreach, email primers, and callback scheduling — to warm contacts before the dial and improve the ratio of live conversations per dial made.

Timing and Life Events Are Creating Temporary Blockers Worth Revisiting

Several prospects flagged genuine situational timing issues — paternity leave, pressing internal projects, being caught at a bad time repeatedly. These are not hard rejections and should be treated as a separate follow-up pipeline. One prospect explicitly requested a callback the following week; another suggested Q1 of the following year. Capturing and actioning these soft-open doors in a structured way could represent a meaningful source of future pipeline with minimal additional prospecting effort.

Recommended Actions
1

Develop a dedicated response to the 'we already have a tool' objection — shift from feature-level comparison to diagnostic questions about where the current solution breaks down under volume or complexity, specifically targeting prospects using ad-hoc AI tools or manual workarounds that may not hold at scale.

2

Build a structured referral-capture process into every disqualification call — when a prospect names a more relevant stakeholder (as Yanis did with Andy Hunter and Stuart Hall), that referral should be logged, verified, and actioned within 48 hours rather than treated as a side note.

3

Create a dedicated 'soft-open' follow-up sequence for prospects who requested callbacks, email outreach, or future re-engagement — tag these by re-contact date (including specific Q1 flags), and ensure outreach is personalised to the context of the original conversation rather than a cold restart.

4

Review and tighten contact list segmentation to filter out non-B2B organisations — prospects who confirmed they do not sell to businesses represent a structurally irrelevant audience for Securilix's proposition and consuming dial capacity that could be redirected to higher-fit targets.

5

Reframe the qualification approach around deal impact and resource burden per questionnaire rather than total questionnaire volume — this will surface higher-intent prospects who are currently self-disqualifying based on frequency while still experiencing significant time loss per instance.

Situation
Mark is the Global Defence & Security Director at Basis Technologies, a small (~180 person) SAP partner company selling SAP software to defence agencies globally. His role is sales-focused — he runs their global defence business and sells software to defence agencies across GB and NATO. The company holds ISO 27001 and Cyber Essentials Plus certifications. Mark is not the CISO or a technical security person — he is a sales leader who encounters security compliance as part of the sales process. He acknowledged there is no dedicated security director at the company, meaning security questionnaire ownership is unclear internally.
Pain
Mark immediately recognised what security questionnaires are and showed genuine intrigue, suggesting this is a real and familiar friction point. The company operates in the defence and regulated sector, meaning they are likely subject to demanding compliance requirements including FedRAMP in the US, NATO-related accreditations, and defence-specific clearance frameworks — all of which generate complex, time-consuming questionnaires. Mark could not clearly identify who internally would own this problem ('I can't quite see who I would push you onto'), which itself signals a pain point: there is no clear process or ownership for handling inbound security questionnaires.
Impact
No specific revenue figures or deal losses were cited, but the context is high-stakes: Basis Technologies sells into defence agencies across NATO and the US, where procurement processes are heavily compliance-driven. The SDR's framing — that questionnaires can take 3–7 days or weeks to complete, leaving competitors to submit RFIs and RFPs first — clearly resonated with Mark, who did not push back on it. In a defence sales context, losing ground in a competitive RFP cycle due to slow compliance responses could mean losing significant government or enterprise contracts. The risk is both competitive (being slower than rivals) and reputational (appearing unprepared to regulated buyers).
Critical Event
No hard deadline or specific regulatory trigger was explicitly mentioned. However, Mark referenced several compliance frameworks relevant to their business — FedRAMP, NATO accreditations, Cyber Essentials Plus, ISO 27001 — which suggests ongoing and evolving compliance obligations. He said 'there are things that are coming up' without specifying what those are. The AE should probe this directly on the Monday call to determine whether there is an upcoming procurement, audit, accreditation renewal, or new defence contract pursuit driving urgency.
Decision
Mark is a sales leader, not a technical or security decision-maker. He was candid that he is 'not in any way, shape or form' the security questionnaire owner. He struggled to identify who internally would be responsible, which suggests either the function is distributed or underdeveloped. He expressed personal curiosity and said it was 'worth investigation,' implying he could be an internal champion but is unlikely to be the sole decision-maker or budget holder. The AE needs to uncover who handles compliance and security documentation at Basis Technologies — likely someone in IT, InfoSec, or operations — and determine whether Mark has the influence or appetite to bring them into the conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by acknowledging the rushed nature of the first call and thank Mark for making time on a Monday. Briefly recap what was discussed to show continuity — reference that he mentioned ISO 27001, Cyber Essentials Plus, FedRAMP, and NATO accreditations specifically, so he knows the AE has been properly briefed and isn't starting from scratch. The strongest angle here is the defence sales cycle: Mark is a sales director selling into defence agencies, so frame Securilix's value in terms of competitive speed in RFP and RFI processes — losing ground to a competitor because a questionnaire took two weeks to complete is a tangible, credible risk in his world. Avoid leading with technical compliance language; lead with sales velocity and deal risk. Early in the call, probe the 'things that are coming up' comment — ask directly whether there is a specific contract pursuit, accreditation renewal, or new market entry (e.g. FedRAMP for US defence work) that is making this timely. This is the most likely route to surfacing a Critical Event. Separately, help Mark identify the right internal stakeholder — suggest that in companies without a dedicated security director, it is often the IT manager, bid manager, or head of operations who ends up owning questionnaire responses. Offer to involve that person in a follow-up if appropriate. Do not oversell on this first meeting — Mark's interest is genuine but exploratory. The goal is to confirm the pain is real, find the internal owner, and identify whether there is a near-term trigger that justifies moving forward.

She described the situation as something that has been on her list for 'a very long time' with no resolution in sight, suggesting this is a sustained operational drag rather than a temporary spike.
Critical Event
No hard deadline, regulatory trigger, or budget cycle was mentioned explicitly. However, the current volume — three questionnaires in one week, 16 already in 2026 by mid-May — suggests the problem is live and acute right now rather than theoretical. The backlog of internal audits and security updates she referenced implies there is mounting pressure building alongside the questionnaire workload. No specific event was cited that would force a decision by a particular date.
Decision
Karen appeared to be the primary decision-maker and the sole user of any solution — she owns the problem entirely and booked the meeting on her own authority without mentioning needing sign-off from anyone else. No budget figure, procurement process, or additional stakeholders were referenced. The decision process appears straightforward at this stage, but this has not been confirmed. It is worth the AE probing whether there is a line manager, finance approval, or procurement step involved before any commercial conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by referencing the specifics Karen shared on the call — do not make her repeat herself. She explicitly said she would not hold Sean to remembering everything, but she will respond well to an AE who clearly has been thoroughly briefed. Start with something like: 'I understand you had one questionnaire alone that took four days, and you are currently managing three at once — I want to make sure we are showing you something that actually fits the complexity you are dealing with, not just the straightforward cases.' This signals you have listened and sets the right tone.

The demo must address her four specific scenarios directly: (1) portal-based questionnaires — be explicit about what Securilix can and cannot do here; (2) project-specific responses that require internal alignment before answers can be confirmed — show how the human review layer accommodates this rather than just asserting it does; (3) documents attached by clients requiring formal confirmation of agreement — come with a clear answer, not a vague 'we can look into it'; (4) policy redaction and document sensitivity — explain the confidentiality model and what access Securilix requires.

Probe on volume trajectory. She had 16 questionnaires by mid-May 2026, with five each in February and March. Ask whether she expects this to grow as Just After Midnight wins more business, particularly in new regions — this could strengthen the commercial case significantly.

Probe on decision-making. She booked independently but no one else was mentioned. Ask directly: 'Is this something you would be in a position to move forward on yourself, or would others need to be involved?' Do this early to avoid a late-stage stall.

Quantify the time cost together during the meeting. She mentioned four days on one questionnaire, 326 questions on another, and a 150-page T&C document on a third. Work with her to estimate total hours lost per month — this will make the ROI conversation concrete and personal rather than theoretical.

Do not oversell automation. Karen is sophisticated and already understands the limitations of pure AI responses. She will be sceptical of anything that sounds like a generic AI tool. Lead with the human oversight and customisation angle, and use the AI tooling as a supporting capability rather than the headline.
