Campaign Overview
May 2026 was a month defined by a crowded and increasingly self-sufficient market. The overall tone of conversations was polite but firm — prospects were not hostile, but many had already arrived at their own solutions to the security questionnaire problem, whether through dedicated software, AI tooling, or internally built workarounds. The pipeline that did open was meaningful, but reaching it required navigating a market where the pain point Securilix addresses is well understood and, in many cases, already being acted upon independently.

Common Objections
Already have a tool or solution in place ~6 conversations
"They already have a tool in place that handles most of the heavy lifting, so our solution isn't relevant for them currently."

This was the dominant objection of the month and reflects a maturing market. Prospects are not dismissing the problem — they have simply already invested in addressing it. The opportunity here lies in probing the quality and completeness of existing solutions: how well does their current tool handle nuanced or high-volume questionnaires, and what does it cost them in time and human oversight? Positioning Securilix around gaps, limitations, and total cost of ownership rather than the problem itself may unlock more of these conversations.

We don't receive enough questionnaires for it to matter ~3 conversations
"They receive security questionnaires infrequently (2-6 times every few months). They have found a workaround, indicating it's not a significant issue for them."

A subset of prospects are not experiencing the pain at sufficient volume to feel urgency. This is a qualification signal rather than a true objection — these prospects may not be the right ICP at this stage. However, it is worth probing whether low volume is a genuine market reality for their business or a symptom of a sales process that is quietly losing deals upstream due to questionnaire friction they are not attributing correctly.

Not the right person / not my area ~5 conversations
"The security questionnaires aren't his area. He suggested I reach out to either Andy Hunter (CSO) or Stuart Hall (main DRC engineer) for the best assistance."

A meaningful number of calls landed with individuals who were adjacent to but not responsible for security questionnaire management. This is partly a data quality issue and partly a structural one — ownership of this problem is not always obvious from a job title. The positive outcome in some cases was a direct referral to named contacts, which should be treated as a warm lead pathway and tracked accordingly.

Not a good time / too busy right now ~4 conversations
"He suggested I send information via email and follow up in Q1 next year to see if it's worth discussing further."

Several prospects deflected with timing rather than a substantive rejection. These are not closed doors — they are deferred conversations. The risk is that by Q1 next year, these prospects may have self-solved or moved budget elsewhere. A structured follow-up sequence in the near term, rather than waiting until the suggested date, may be more effective at capturing these prospects before they close the gap themselves.

We don't sell to businesses, so questionnaires aren't relevant ~1 conversations
"They don't receive security questionnaires as they don't sell to businesses, so my solution isn't relevant."

This objection points to a targeting issue at the data level. Prospects who sell exclusively to consumers or SMBs without enterprise procurement processes will not experience security questionnaire volume. Filtering target lists for B2B-facing, enterprise-selling organisations with defined sales cycles would reduce wasted dials in this category.

It would be a waste of time ~1 conversations
"The prospect was resistant and felt it would be a waste of time."

This blunt response, while rare, suggests a failure to establish relevance quickly enough in the opening of the call. When a prospect cannot immediately see how the conversation connects to a real cost or friction they experience, dismissal is fast. A sharper, more specific opening that names a concrete outcome — such as hours saved per questionnaire or deals accelerated — may reduce this type of early disengagement.

What Prospects Asked
Can you send information over by email instead? ~2 conversations

This request, while often a soft deflection, also reflects a preference for evaluating solutions asynchronously before committing time to a conversation. It suggests these prospects are not opposed to the idea but need more context before engaging. Email follow-up in these cases should be concise, specific to their stated situation, and include a clear call to action rather than a generic brochure-style message.

Can you reach me on LinkedIn instead? ~1 conversations

The preference for LinkedIn over a live call indicates a prospect who is open to connection but on their own terms. This is a channel preference worth honouring — a well-crafted LinkedIn message referencing the call context and framing a specific question or insight relevant to their role is more likely to progress than a generic connection request.

Can you call back at a better time? ~2 conversations

Callback requests indicate genuine availability constraints rather than avoidance. These prospects represent live pipeline and should be treated as priority re-contacts with a specific agreed time wherever possible. The risk of vague callback agreements is that they stall — pinning down a day and time on the call itself significantly improves conversion on follow-up.

Market Themes
The market is solving the problem without us

The most striking pattern across May was the number of prospects who had already taken action on their security questionnaire burden — through tools like Vanta, AI automation, trust centres, or internal processes. This is not apathy; it is a market that has registered the pain and moved to resolve it. For Securilix, the implication is that messaging needs to evolve from problem-awareness to differentiation — the conversation can no longer start with 'do you have this problem?' but must instead start with 'here is what your current approach is probably missing.'

Timing and access are significant conversion barriers

A large proportion of dials this month did not result in substantive conversations — not because of outright rejection, but because of gatekeepers, automated systems, unavailable contacts, and poor timing. Where human conversations did occur, many were cut short by competing priorities or caught at genuinely inconvenient moments. This suggests that cadence design — the number of attempts, the time of day, and the sequencing of call, email, and LinkedIn — is as important as the quality of the conversation itself.

Referrals within accounts are an underutilised asset

Several calls that did not reach the intended contact still produced named referrals to more relevant decision-makers — CSOs, DRC engineers, and compliance leads. These warm hand-offs represent a meaningful pipeline pathway that is currently not being fully exploited. Systematically capturing and acting on referral names, rather than treating them as incidental, could materially improve the quality of contacts reached in subsequent months.

Ownership of security questionnaires is fragmented

Across conversations, responsibility for security questionnaires was attributed variously to IT, compliance teams, sales, and engineering — often with uncertainty within the same organisation. This fragmentation means that reaching one person with the right title does not guarantee reaching the decision-maker. Multi-threaded outreach into target accounts, covering both technical and commercial stakeholders, is likely to improve both connection rates and meeting quality.

Low questionnaire volume is masking latent pain

Some prospects dismissed the relevance of the solution on the basis that they simply do not receive many questionnaires. However, low inbound questionnaire volume can itself be a symptom of deals stalling or being abandoned before formal evaluation — pain that is not being attributed correctly by the business. Reframing the conversation around revenue impact and deal velocity, rather than questionnaire volume alone, may surface latency pain that prospects are not currently connecting to the problem.

Recommended Actions
1

Develop a specific rebuttal track for 'we already have a tool' — rather than accepting this at face value, build a set of probing questions that surface the limitations of existing solutions (e.g. time spent on manual review, accuracy issues, questionnaire complexity not covered by automation). The goal is to shift the conversation from 'do you have a tool?' to 'how well is it actually working at scale?'

2

Capture and action all named referrals from calls as a priority follow-up category — prospects such as Andy Hunter (CSO) and Stuart Hall (DRC engineer), mentioned by Yanis, represent warm, relevant contacts that should be contacted with explicit reference to the referral source. Build a tracking process to ensure none of these leads are lost between call logging and outreach.

3

Review and tighten the target contact list to prioritise B2B-facing organisations with active enterprise sales cycles — contacts who confirmed they do not sell to businesses, or who are in consumer-facing roles, are not viable prospects for the Securilix proposition and their removal will improve the efficiency of dials and conversation quality.

4

Design a structured short-cycle follow-up sequence for all 'call me back' and 'email me' responses — rather than waiting until a vaguely agreed future date, deploy a three-touch sequence (email, LinkedIn, call) within two weeks to maintain momentum before prospects self-solve or disengage entirely.

5

Test a revised call opening that leads with a specific, quantified outcome (e.g. time saved per questionnaire, or days removed from a sales cycle) rather than a problem statement — the current market has already registered the problem, so opening with differentiation and value is likely to reduce early-call dismissals and increase the proportion of calls that reach a substantive conversation.

Situation
Mark is the Global Defence & Security Director at Basis Technologies, a small (~180 person) SAP partner company selling SAP software to defence agencies globally. His role is sales-focused — he runs their global defence business and sells software to defence agencies across GB and NATO. The company holds ISO 27001 and Cyber Essentials Plus certifications. Mark is not the CISO or a technical security person — he is a sales leader who encounters security compliance as part of the sales process. He acknowledged there is no dedicated security director at the company, meaning security questionnaire ownership is unclear internally.
Pain
Mark immediately recognised what security questionnaires are and showed genuine intrigue, suggesting this is a real and familiar friction point. The company operates in the defence and regulated sector, meaning they are likely subject to demanding compliance requirements including FedRAMP in the US, NATO-related accreditations, and defence-specific clearance frameworks — all of which generate complex, time-consuming questionnaires. Mark could not clearly identify who internally would own this problem ('I can't quite see who I would push you onto'), which itself signals a pain point: there is no clear process or ownership for handling inbound security questionnaires.
Impact
No specific revenue figures or deal losses were cited, but the context is high-stakes: Basis Technologies sells into defence agencies across NATO and the US, where procurement processes are heavily compliance-driven. The SDR's framing — that questionnaires can take 3–7 days or weeks to complete, leaving competitors to submit RFIs and RFPs first — clearly resonated with Mark, who did not push back on it. In a defence sales context, losing ground in a competitive RFP cycle due to slow compliance responses could mean losing significant government or enterprise contracts. The risk is both competitive (being slower than rivals) and reputational (appearing unprepared to regulated buyers).
Critical Event
No hard deadline or specific regulatory trigger was explicitly mentioned. However, Mark referenced several compliance frameworks relevant to their business — FedRAMP, NATO accreditations, Cyber Essentials Plus, ISO 27001 — which suggests ongoing and evolving compliance obligations. He said 'there are things that are coming up' without specifying what those are. The AE should probe this directly on the Monday call to determine whether there is an upcoming procurement, audit, accreditation renewal, or new defence contract pursuit driving urgency.
Decision
Mark is a sales leader, not a technical or security decision-maker. He was candid that he is 'not in any way, shape or form' the security questionnaire owner. He struggled to identify who internally would be responsible, which suggests either the function is distributed or underdeveloped. He expressed personal curiosity and said it was 'worth investigation,' implying he could be an internal champion but is unlikely to be the sole decision-maker or budget holder. The AE needs to uncover who handles compliance and security documentation at Basis Technologies — likely someone in IT, InfoSec, or operations — and determine whether Mark has the influence or appetite to bring them into the conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by acknowledging the rushed nature of the first call and thank Mark for making time on a Monday. Briefly recap what was discussed to show continuity — reference that he mentioned ISO 27001, Cyber Essentials Plus, FedRAMP, and NATO accreditations specifically, so he knows the AE has been properly briefed and isn't starting from scratch. The strongest angle here is the defence sales cycle: Mark is a sales director selling into defence agencies, so frame Securilix's value in terms of competitive speed in RFP and RFI processes — losing ground to a competitor because a questionnaire took two weeks to complete is a tangible, credible risk in his world. Avoid leading with technical compliance language; lead with sales velocity and deal risk. Early in the call, probe the 'things that are coming up' comment — ask directly whether there is a specific contract pursuit, accreditation renewal, or new market entry (e.g. FedRAMP for US defence work) that is making this timely. This is the most likely route to surfacing a Critical Event. Separately, help Mark identify the right internal stakeholder — suggest that in companies without a dedicated security director, it is often the IT manager, bid manager, or head of operations who ends up owning questionnaire responses. Offer to involve that person in a follow-up if appropriate. Do not oversell on this first meeting — Mark's interest is genuine but exploratory. The goal is to confirm the pain is real, find the internal owner, and identify whether there is a near-term trigger that justifies moving forward.

Pain

A core frustration is the nuance required: many questionnaires are project-specific, requiring internal conversations to confirm what services are actually being provided before she can answer accurately. Others arrive via portals with requirements to upload supporting documents, redact sensitive policies, or confirm agreement with the client's own attached documentation. She also flags that questionnaires arrive while she is in external audits with do-not-disturb active, and stakeholders still demand same-day or next-day turnaround.
Impact
The questionnaire burden is directly crowding out Karen's core responsibilities. She explicitly listed internal auditing and security process updates as work that has fallen behind. The time cost is significant — four days on a single questionnaire is not unusual for her. There is also a sales cycle implication: Just After Midnight's ability to close deals is dependent on Karen completing these responses, and delays or errors could jeopardise contracts. The regional and jurisdictional complexity adds compliance risk — each new geography brings unfamiliar legislation she must interpret before she can respond. She described the situation as something that has been on her list for 'a very long time' with no resolution in sight, suggesting this is a sustained operational drag rather than a temporary spike.
Critical Event
No hard deadline, regulatory trigger, or budget cycle was mentioned explicitly. However, the current volume — three questionnaires in one week, 16 already in 2026 by mid-May — suggests the problem is live and acute right now rather than theoretical. The backlog of internal audits and security updates she referenced implies there is mounting pressure building alongside the questionnaire workload. No specific event was cited that would force a decision by a particular date.
Decision
Karen appeared to be the primary decision-maker and the sole user of any solution — she owns the problem entirely and booked the meeting on her own authority without mentioning needing sign-off from anyone else. No budget figure, procurement process, or additional stakeholders were referenced. The decision process appears straightforward at this stage, but this has not been confirmed. It is worth the AE probing whether there is a line manager, finance approval, or procurement step involved before any commercial conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by referencing the specifics Karen shared on the call — do not make her repeat herself. She explicitly said she would not hold Sean to remembering everything, but she will respond well to an AE who clearly has been thoroughly briefed. Start with something like: 'I understand you had one questionnaire alone that took four days, and you are currently managing three at once — I want to make sure we are showing you something that actually fits the complexity you are dealing with, not just the straightforward cases.' This signals you have listened and sets the right tone.

The demo must address her four specific scenarios directly: (1) portal-based questionnaires — be explicit about what Securilix can and cannot do here; (2) project-specific responses that require internal alignment before answers can be confirmed — show how the human review layer accommodates this rather than just asserting it does; (3) documents attached by clients requiring formal confirmation of agreement — come with a clear answer, not a vague 'we can look into it'; (4) policy redaction and document sensitivity — explain the confidentiality model and what access Securilix requires.

Probe on volume trajectory. She had 16 questionnaires by mid-May 2026, with five each in February and March. Ask whether she expects this to grow as Just After Midnight wins more business, particularly in new regions — this could strengthen the commercial case significantly.

Probe on decision-making. She booked independently but no one else was mentioned. Ask directly: 'Is this something you would be in a position to move forward on yourself, or would others need to be involved?' Do this early to avoid a late-stage stall.

Quantify the time cost together during the meeting. She mentioned four days on one questionnaire, 326 questions on another, and a 150-page T&C document on a third. Work with her to estimate total hours lost per month — this will make the ROI conversation concrete and personal rather than theoretical.

Do not oversell automation. Karen is sophisticated and already understands the limitations of pure AI responses. She will be sceptical of anything that sounds like a generic AI tool. Lead with the human oversight and customisation angle, and use the AI tooling as a supporting capability rather than the headline.
