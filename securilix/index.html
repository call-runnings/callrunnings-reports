Campaign Overview
May 2026 was a month defined more by market education than market readiness. The majority of conversations revealed a prospect base that is broadly aware of the security questionnaire problem but has — rightly or wrongly — convinced itself the problem is already solved. Whether through homegrown workarounds, recently adopted AI tools, or simply a low enough volume of inbound questionnaires to feel the pain acutely, many prospects we reached this month had mentally closed the door before the conversation had a chance to open. That said, two meetings were booked and seven prospects were activated, which tells us there is genuine interest in the market — it simply requires more precise targeting and sharper qualification to surface it consistently.

Common Objections
Already have a tool or solution in place ~5 conversations
"We already have a tool in place that handles most of the heavy lifting."

This was the single most common substantive objection of the month. Prospects are not dismissing the category — they are claiming it is already addressed. This represents an opportunity, not a dead end. The right response is to probe what 'handled' actually means: volume managed, accuracy rate, time spent per questionnaire, and whether the tool covers edge cases. In most cases, these solutions are partial or recently adopted and have not yet been properly stress-tested. Securilix's messaging should shift towards displacement positioning — not 'do you have a problem' but 'how confident are you that your current solution is doing the whole job'.

We don't receive enough questionnaires for it to matter ~4 conversations
"They receive security questionnaires infrequently — 2 to 6 times every few months. They have found a workaround, indicating it's not a significant issue for them."

Low-volume prospects are self-selecting out on the basis of frequency rather than impact. The counter-argument is that even a small number of poorly handled questionnaires can stall or lose a significant enterprise deal. Messaging should focus on the cost-per-questionnaire and the deal risk associated with slow or incomplete responses, rather than volume alone. This objection may also indicate poor targeting — organisations with low questionnaire volume are likely earlier-stage or operating in less regulated verticals.

Not relevant to our business model — we don't sell to enterprises ~3 conversations
"They don't receive security questionnaires as they don't sell to businesses, so the solution isn't relevant."

This is a genuine disqualification and not an objection to overcome. It points to an ICP refinement opportunity — companies that sell exclusively to consumers or small businesses are unlikely to face inbound security questionnaire pressure. Tightening the target list to organisations with enterprise or mid-market B2B sales motions would reduce wasted dials in this category.

Not my area — redirected to another contact ~4 conversations
"The security questionnaires aren't his area. He suggested I reach out to either Andy Hunter (CSO) or Stuart Hall (main DRC engineer)."

Several prospects confirmed genuine organisational relevance but redirected the conversation to a more appropriate stakeholder. This is a positive signal dressed as a deflection. Each referral should be treated as a warm lead and followed up with a personalised approach that references the original contact. Where names were provided — such as Andy Hunter and Stuart Hall — these should be actioned as priority dials in June.

Bad timing — busy, on leave, or mid-project ~4 conversations
"He's currently on paternity leave and not focused on work matters. They're in a brief EMF stage and not dealing with enterprise issues."

Timing objections clustered this month around genuine situational reasons rather than avoidance. These are not closed doors. Barry Shaw agreed to a callback the following week. Prefers async / non-phone contact ~2 conversations

A small but notable segment of prospects are not opposed to the proposition but are resistant to the cold call format specifically. LinkedIn follow-up should be actioned immediately after these calls, while the conversation is fresh. What Prospects Asked

Prospects requesting email follow-up are not disengaging — they are moving the evaluation to their own pace. This is a mild buying signal and indicates openness to the proposition, but a preference for lower-pressure evaluation. The email sent in response should be sharp, specific, and contain a clear next step rather than a product brochure. It should reference the exact point raised in the call to demonstrate the conversation was genuinely listened to.

Who are you and what does your company do? ~5 conversations

The frequency of basic qualification questions from gatekeepers and receptionists suggests that Securilix is not yet a recognised name in many of the target organisations. This is expected at this stage of a campaign but worth noting — a brief, memorable one-line description of what Securilix does and who it helps will make a material difference to gatekeeper pass-through rates.

Can I call you back / is there a better time? ~3 conversations

Prospects asking for a callback or alternative timing are demonstrating that the problem space is not irrelevant — they simply cannot engage at the moment of the call. These should be logged with a specific agreed follow-up date and not allowed to go cold. The follow-up call should open by referencing the original conversation and the agreed timing to establish credibility and continuity.

Market Themes
The market is self-solving — but imperfectly

The dominant theme of May was prospects who believe they have already addressed the security questionnaire problem, either through AI tools, existing software, or internal workarounds. However, the nature of these solutions — mixed approaches, home-baked tools, partially automated processes — suggests that many of these fixes are fragile or incomplete. The opportunity for Securilix lies in positioning against the false confidence of 'good enough' solutions before those solutions visibly fail.

Reaching the right person remains the primary conversion constraint

A substantial share of dials this month did not reach the intended decision-maker, either because the contact was unavailable, the number connected to an automated system, or the wrong person answered entirely. In the cases where the right individual was reached, conversations were substantive and productive. Improving the accuracy of contact data and the persistence of follow-up attempts would likely have a disproportionate impact on conversion rates.

Security questionnaire ownership is fragmented within organisations

Multiple calls this month surfaced the fact that security questionnaire responsibility is not uniformly assigned. Some organisations route it through compliance teams, others through sales, others through engineering functions such as dedicated DRC engineers. This fragmentation means that reaching one person in the right company is not enough — understanding the ownership structure before the call, and having a referral strategy ready, will improve the quality of each connected conversation.

Build-versus-buy decisions are live and interruptible

At least two prospects this month were actively constructing their own solutions — one building a public trust centre, another developing a home-baked tool. These are not lost prospects; they are in the middle of a decision that Securilix can still influence. Prospects in active build phases are often underestimating the ongoing maintenance and scalability challenges of proprietary solutions. A targeted message around total cost of ownership and time-to-value could be highly effective with this cohort.

Volume misconception is suppressing perceived need

Several prospects discounted the relevance of Securilix's offering on the basis that they 'don't get many' security questionnaires. This suggests that the current market messaging may be inadvertently framing the problem as a volume challenge rather than a risk and revenue challenge. Repositioning around the cost of a single delayed or failed questionnaire response — in terms of lost deals and reputational risk — would reframe the conversation for lower-volume prospects who currently self-disqualify.

Situation

Pain
Mark immediately recognised what security questionnaires are and showed genuine intrigue, suggesting this is a real and familiar friction point. The company operates in the defence and regulated sector, meaning they are likely subject to demanding compliance requirements including FedRAMP in the US, NATO-related accreditations, and defence-specific clearance frameworks — all of which generate complex, time-consuming questionnaires. Mark could not clearly identify who internally would own this problem ('I can't quite see who I would push you onto'), which itself signals a pain point: there is no clear process or ownership for handling inbound security questionnaires.
Impact
No specific revenue figures or deal losses were cited, but the context is high-stakes: Basis Technologies sells into defence agencies across NATO and the US, where procurement processes are heavily compliance-driven. The SDR's framing — that questionnaires can take 3–7 days or weeks to complete, leaving competitors to submit RFIs and RFPs first — clearly resonated with Mark, who did not push back on it. In a defence sales context, losing ground in a competitive RFP cycle due to slow compliance responses could mean losing significant government or enterprise contracts. The risk is both competitive (being slower than rivals) and reputational (appearing unprepared to regulated buyers).
Critical Event
No hard deadline or specific regulatory trigger was explicitly mentioned. However, Mark referenced several compliance frameworks relevant to their business — FedRAMP, NATO accreditations, Cyber Essentials Plus, ISO 27001 — which suggests ongoing and evolving compliance obligations. He said 'there are things that are coming up' without specifying what those are. The AE should probe this directly on the Monday call to determine whether there is an upcoming procurement, audit, accreditation renewal, or new defence contract pursuit driving urgency.
Decision
Mark is a sales leader, not a technical or security decision-maker. He was candid that he is 'not in any way, shape or form' the security questionnaire owner. He struggled to identify who internally would be responsible, which suggests either the function is distributed or underdeveloped. He expressed personal curiosity and said it was 'worth investigation,' implying he could be an internal champion but is unlikely to be the sole decision-maker or budget holder. The AE needs to uncover who handles compliance and security documentation at Basis Technologies — likely someone in IT, InfoSec, or operations — and determine whether Mark has the influence or appetite to bring them into the conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by acknowledging the rushed nature of the first call and thank Mark for making time on a Monday. Briefly recap what was discussed to show continuity — reference that he mentioned ISO 27001, Cyber Essentials Plus, FedRAMP, and NATO accreditations specifically, so he knows the AE has been properly briefed and isn't starting from scratch. The strongest angle here is the defence sales cycle: Mark is a sales director selling into defence agencies, so frame Securilix's value in terms of competitive speed in RFP and RFI processes — losing ground to a competitor because a questionnaire took two weeks to complete is a tangible, credible risk in his world. Avoid leading with technical compliance language; lead with sales velocity and deal risk. Early in the call, probe the 'things that are coming up' comment — ask directly whether there is a specific contract pursuit, accreditation renewal, or new market entry (e.g. FedRAMP for US defence work) that is making this timely. This is the most likely route to surfacing a Critical Event. Separately, help Mark identify the right internal stakeholder — suggest that in companies without a dedicated security director, it is often the IT manager, bid manager, or head of operations who ends up owning questionnaire responses. Offer to involve that person in a follow-up if appropriate. Do not oversell on this first meeting — Mark's interest is genuine but exploratory. The goal is to confirm the pain is real, find the internal owner, and identify whether there is a near-term trigger that justifies moving forward.

The regional and jurisdictional complexity adds compliance risk — each new geography brings unfamiliar legislation she must interpret before she can respond. She described the situation as something that has been on her list for 'a very long time' with no resolution in sight, suggesting this is a sustained operational drag rather than a temporary spike.
Critical Event
No hard deadline, regulatory trigger, or budget cycle was mentioned explicitly. However, the current volume — three questionnaires in one week, 16 already in 2026 by mid-May — suggests the problem is live and acute right now rather than theoretical. The backlog of internal audits and security updates she referenced implies there is mounting pressure building alongside the questionnaire workload. No specific event was cited that would force a decision by a particular date.
Decision
Karen appeared to be the primary decision-maker and the sole user of any solution — she owns the problem entirely and booked the meeting on her own authority without mentioning needing sign-off from anyone else. No budget figure, procurement process, or additional stakeholders were referenced. The decision process appears straightforward at this stage, but this has not been confirmed. It is worth the AE probing whether there is a line manager, finance approval, or procurement step involved before any commercial conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by referencing the specifics Karen shared on the call — do not make her repeat herself. She explicitly said she would not hold Sean to remembering everything, but she will respond well to an AE who clearly has been thoroughly briefed. Start with something like: 'I understand you had one questionnaire alone that took four days, and you are currently managing three at once — I want to make sure we are showing you something that actually fits the complexity you are dealing with, not just the straightforward cases.' Probe on decision-making. She booked independently but no one else was mentioned. Ask directly: 'Is this something you would be in a position to move forward on yourself, or would others need to be involved?' Do this early to avoid a late-stage stall.

Quantify the time cost together during the meeting. She mentioned four days on one questionnaire, 326 questions on another, and a 150-page T&C document on a third. Work with her to estimate total hours lost per month — this will make the ROI conversation concrete and personal rather than theoretical.

Do not oversell automation. Karen is sophisticated and already understands the limitations of pure AI responses. She will be sceptical of anything that sounds like a generic AI tool. Lead with the human oversight and customisation angle, and use the AI tooling as a supporting capability rather than the headline.
