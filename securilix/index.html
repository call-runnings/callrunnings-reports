Campaign Overview
May 2026 was a month defined by persistent gatekeeping, a high volume of wrong contacts, and a market that — where it did engage — revealed it has largely found ways to cope with the security questionnaire problem on its own terms. Conversations were hard-won, and when they happened, the response from prospects was most often that they had already built or bought something to handle the challenge. That said, the market is clearly aware of the problem Securilix solves, which means the positioning is landing on familiar ears rather than deaf ones — the challenge is less about education and more about displacement.

Common Objections
Already have a solution in place ~6 conversations
"We already have a tool in place that handles most of the heavy lifting."

This was the single most common substantive objection across the month. Prospects are not unaware of the problem — they have simply resolved it, or believe they have. The opportunity here is to probe the quality and completeness of those solutions rather than accept the objection at face value. Questions around accuracy rates, time still spent, and whether sales cycles are genuinely faster could expose cracks in their confidence.

Not relevant — we don't receive questionnaires ~4 conversations
"They don't receive security questionnaires as they don't sell to businesses, so my solution isn't relevant."

A portion of the contact list includes businesses that simply do not operate in a context where security questionnaires arise — they sell to consumers or do not engage in enterprise procurement. This is a targeting issue. Filtering for B2B SaaS companies, particularly those selling into regulated industries, would significantly reduce wasted dials on this front.

Low volume — not a pressing problem ~3 conversations
"They receive security questionnaires infrequently (2-6 times every few months). They have found a workaround, indicating it's not a significant issue for them."

For prospects receiving very few questionnaires, the ROI case for a dedicated solution is harder to make. These contacts are not worth pursuing aggressively. However, volume perceptions can shift quickly with sales growth — a light-touch nurture sequence positioning Securilix as the solution to have before scale becomes the right play.

Bad timing / not the right moment ~3 conversations
"He suggested I send information via email and follow up in Q1 next year to see if it's worth discussing further."

Several prospects acknowledged relevance but pushed back on timing — citing paternity leave, competing priorities, or early-stage company phases. These are not hard nos. They represent a defined follow-up pipeline that should be tracked with specific re-contact dates. The Q1 mention in particular signals a budget or planning cycle alignment worth noting.

Not my area — wrong person ~5 conversations
"Yanis indicated that the security questionnaires aren't his area. He suggested I reach out to either Andy Hunter (CSO) or Stuart Hall (main DRC engineer)."

A meaningful number of calls landed on people who recognised the problem but did not own it. Where referrals were given, they were specific and useful — these should be acted on immediately. Where no referral was offered, it is worth revisiting the contact data to identify whether CSO, Head of Security, or Sales Engineering functions are being targeted consistently.

What Prospects Asked
Can you send information over by email instead? ~3 conversations

Prospects asking to be emailed rather than continuing the conversation are not necessarily disengaged — they are deferring evaluation to a moment of their choosing. This signals they want to assess on their own terms. A tightly focused one-page email that leads with displacement of their current workaround, rather than a generic overview, is likely to perform better than a standard follow-up sequence.

Who are you and why are you calling? ~4 conversations

This appeared frequently in gatekeeper interactions and with prospects who did not recognise the number. It is less a question of genuine curiosity and more an indication that the opening statement needs to establish relevance faster. A crisper, problem-first opener — naming the specific pain before naming the company — would reduce the friction at this stage.

Is this relevant to us given how few questionnaires we receive? ~2 conversations

Prospects are self-qualifying against volume, which means they are applying the wrong filter. The conversation needs to reframe value around sales cycle speed and deal risk, not questionnaire count. Even one delayed or lost deal due to a slow questionnaire response justifies the solution — that reframe should be built into the pitch for these objections.

Market Themes
The DIY wave: prospects are building before they buy

A notable pattern this month was prospects actively mid-build on their own solutions — public trust centres, home-baked tools, and AI overlays cobbled together from existing platforms. This is not a permanent closed door; it is a window before they discover the limitations of what they have built. Timing follow-ups to coincide with six to nine months post-conversation — when the cracks in those solutions typically begin to show — is a high-value strategy.

AI has become a default deflection

Multiple prospects used 'we use AI for that now' as a conversation-ender, suggesting that the concept of AI-assisted questionnaire completion has entered mainstream awareness as a perceived fix. This needs to be met directly in the pitch — not by disputing AI, but by addressing what AI alone does not solve, such as accuracy, liability, version control, and integration with sales workflows. Securilix's positioning relative to generic AI use is currently an unanswered question in these calls.

Ownership of the problem is fragmented across organisations

Across the month, questionnaire responsibility was attributed variously to sales, security, compliance, and engineering teams. This fragmentation means that landing on the right person is genuinely difficult, and that the person who feels the pain most acutely (often in sales or pre-sales) is rarely the person who holds the budget. Messaging that speaks to sales velocity — rather than purely security operations — may open more initial doors, with a handoff narrative built in for the technical or compliance buyer.

Referrals given are specific and credible

On the occasions where a prospect was willing to redirect, they named actual people with actual titles — a CSO, a DRC engineer — rather than offering vague signposting. This suggests that where the proposition is understood, contacts do recognise which colleague owns it. These named referrals are among the warmest leads in the entire month's pipeline and should be treated as priority follow-ups with a warm introduction framing.

Data quality is suppressing conversion

A substantial portion of calls this month hit automated systems, wrong numbers, consumer-facing businesses, and contacts on personal lines. This is not a messaging problem — it is a list problem. The effort required to reach a genuine conversation is being inflated by avoidable misdials. Tightening the ICP criteria to focus on B2B SaaS companies in regulated sectors, with confirmed IT or security leadership contacts, would meaningfully improve the ratio of conversations to dials.

Recommended Actions
1

Prioritise immediate follow-up on all named referrals gathered this month — specifically Andy Hunter (CSO) and Stuart Hall (DRC engineer) — using the referring contact's name to open the conversation with a warm frame rather than a cold introduction.

2

Build a displacement sequence for the 'already using AI' objection: develop a concise, evidence-led reframe that acknowledges AI use but highlights the specific gaps it does not close — accuracy accountability, sales cycle data, version control, and enterprise audit trails — to be deployed in both calls and follow-up emails.

3

Conduct a contact list audit before June calling begins: remove or deprioritise consumer-facing businesses, wrong numbers, and sectors where security questionnaires are structurally absent, and cross-reference remaining contacts against CSO, Head of Security, and Sales Engineering job titles to reduce wasted dials on non-owners.

4

Create a time-deferred follow-up pipeline for prospects who indicated a future window — particularly those who referenced Q1 planning cycles or are currently mid-build on their own solutions — with scheduled re-contact dates and a one-line context note to personalise re-engagement.

5

Refine the opening statement to lead with a specific, named pain point before introducing Securilix — for example, framing around delayed deals or questionnaire bottlenecks in enterprise sales cycles — to reduce the 'who are you and why are you calling' friction at the gatekeeper and prospect level.

Basis Technologies
Mark Holder · Global Defence & Security Director
13/05/2026
Situation
Mark is the Global Defence & Security Director at Basis Technologies, a small (~180 person) SAP partner company selling SAP software to defence agencies globally. His role is sales-focused — he runs their global defence business and sells software to defence agencies across GB and NATO. The company holds ISO 27001 and Cyber Essentials Plus certifications. Mark is not the CISO or a technical security person — he is a sales leader who encounters security compliance as part of the sales process. He acknowledged there is no dedicated security director at the company, meaning security questionnaire ownership is unclear internally.
Pain
Mark immediately recognised what security questionnaires are and showed genuine intrigue, suggesting this is a real and familiar friction point. The company operates in the defence and regulated sector, meaning they are likely subject to demanding compliance requirements including FedRAMP in the US, NATO-related accreditations, and defence-specific clearance frameworks — all of which generate complex, time-consuming questionnaires. Mark could not clearly identify who internally would own this problem ('I can't quite see who I would push you onto'), which itself signals a pain point: there is no clear process or ownership for handling inbound security questionnaires.
Impact
No specific revenue figures or deal losses were cited, but the context is high-stakes: Basis Technologies sells into defence agencies across NATO and the US, where procurement processes are heavily compliance-driven. The SDR's framing — that questionnaires can take 3–7 days or weeks to complete, leaving competitors to submit RFIs and RFPs first — clearly resonated with Mark, who did not push back on it. In a defence sales context, losing ground in a competitive RFP cycle due to slow compliance responses could mean losing significant government or enterprise contracts. The risk is both competitive (being slower than rivals) and reputational (appearing unprepared to regulated buyers).
Critical Event
No hard deadline or specific regulatory trigger was explicitly mentioned. However, Mark referenced several compliance frameworks relevant to their business — FedRAMP, NATO accreditations, Cyber Essentials Plus, ISO 27001 — which suggests ongoing and evolving compliance obligations. He said 'there are things that are coming up' without specifying what those are. The AE should probe this directly on the Monday call to determine whether there is an upcoming procurement, audit, accreditation renewal, or new defence contract pursuit driving urgency.
Decision
Mark is a sales leader, not a technical or security decision-maker. He was candid that he is 'not in any way, shape or form' the security questionnaire owner. He struggled to identify who internally would be responsible, which suggests either the function is distributed or underdeveloped. He expressed personal curiosity and said it was 'worth investigation,' implying he could be an internal champion but is unlikely to be the sole decision-maker or budget holder. The AE needs to uncover who handles compliance and security documentation at Basis Technologies — likely someone in IT, InfoSec, or operations — and determine whether Mark has the influence or appetite to bring them into the conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by acknowledging the rushed nature of the first call and thank Mark for making time on a Monday. Briefly recap what was discussed to show continuity — reference that he mentioned ISO 27001, Cyber Essentials Plus, FedRAMP, and NATO accreditations specifically, so he knows the AE has been properly briefed and isn't starting from scratch. The strongest angle here is the defence sales cycle: Mark is a sales director selling into defence agencies, so frame Securilix's value in terms of competitive speed in RFP and RFI processes — losing ground to a competitor because a questionnaire took two weeks to complete is a tangible, credible risk in his world. Avoid leading with technical compliance language; lead with sales velocity and deal risk. Early in the call, probe the 'things that are coming up' comment — ask directly whether there is a specific contract pursuit, accreditation renewal, or new market entry (e.g. FedRAMP for US defence work) that is making this timely. This is the most likely route to surfacing a Critical Event. Separately, help Mark identify the right internal stakeholder — suggest that in companies without a dedicated security director, it is often the IT manager, bid manager, or head of operations who ends up owning questionnaire responses. Offer to involve that person in a follow-up if appropriate. Do not oversell on this first meeting — Mark's interest is genuine but exploratory. The goal is to confirm the pain is real, find the internal owner, and identify whether there is a near-term trigger that justifies moving forward.

Just After Midnight
Karen Haydon · Head of Information Security and Compliance
13/05/2026
Situation
Karen Haydon is Head of Information Security and Compliance at Just After Midnight, a 24/7 operation (likely a managed services or digital operations company based on the domain). She is the sole owner of security questionnaire responses — there is no team or tooling supporting her. She handles a mix of formats: portal-based questionnaires, standalone documents, RFPs, and due diligence questionnaires (DDQs). She attempted to build an internal answer bank herself but never had time to complete it. No existing third-party solution or trust centre is in place. She receives questionnaires from clients across multiple regions and jurisdictions globally.
Pain
Karen is being buried by security questionnaires — she completed 16 in the year to date by mid-May, including five in February and five in March. She currently has three on the go within a single week. Individual questionnaires range from 10–30 minutes for simple ones up to four full days for complex ones — she cited one that had 326 questions and another that was a full RFP with a 150-page terms and conditions document she had to read in full before responding. A core frustration is the nuance required: many questionnaires are project-specific, requiring internal conversations to confirm what services are actually being provided before she can answer accurately. Others arrive via portals with requirements to upload supporting documents, redact sensitive policies, or confirm agreement with the client's own attached documentation. She also flags that questionnaires arrive while she is in external audits with do-not-disturb active, and stakeholders still demand same-day or next-day turnaround.
Impact
The questionnaire burden is directly crowding out Karen's core responsibilities. She explicitly listed internal auditing and security process updates as work that has fallen behind. The time cost is significant — four days on a single questionnaire is not unusual for her. There is also a sales cycle implication: Just After Midnight's ability to close deals is dependent on Karen completing these responses, and delays or errors could jeopardise contracts. The regional and jurisdictional complexity adds compliance risk — each new geography brings unfamiliar legislation she must interpret before she can respond. She described the situation as something that has been on her list for 'a very long time' with no resolution in sight, suggesting this is a sustained operational drag rather than a temporary spike.
Critical Event
No hard deadline, regulatory trigger, or budget cycle was mentioned explicitly. However, the current volume — three questionnaires in one week, 16 already in 2026 by mid-May — suggests the problem is live and acute right now rather than theoretical. The backlog of internal audits and security updates she referenced implies there is mounting pressure building alongside the questionnaire workload. No specific event was cited that would force a decision by a particular date.
Decision
Karen appeared to be the primary decision-maker and the sole user of any solution — she owns the problem entirely and booked the meeting on her own authority without mentioning needing sign-off from anyone else. No budget figure, procurement process, or additional stakeholders were referenced. The decision process appears straightforward at this stage, but this has not been confirmed. It is worth the AE probing whether there is a line manager, finance approval, or procurement step involved before any commercial conversation.
Recommended AE Approach
Open by referencing the specifics Karen shared on the call — do not make her repeat herself. She explicitly said she would not hold Sean to remembering everything, but she will respond well to an AE who clearly has been thoroughly briefed. Start with something like: 'I understand you had one questionnaire alone that took four days, and you are currently managing three at once — I want to make sure we are showing you something that actually fits the complexity you are dealing with, not just the straightforward cases.' This signals you have listened and sets the right tone.

The demo must address her four specific scenarios directly: (1) portal-based questionnaires — be explicit about what Securilix can and cannot do here; (2) project-specific responses that require internal alignment before answers can be confirmed — show how the human review layer accommodates this rather than just asserting it does; (3) documents attached by clients requiring formal confirmation of agreement — come with a clear answer, not a vague 'we can look into it'; (4) policy redaction and document sensitivity — explain the confidentiality model and what access Securilix requires.

Probe on volume trajectory. She had 16 questionnaires by mid-May 2026, with five each in February and March. Ask whether she expects this to grow as Just After Midnight wins more business, particularly in new regions — this could strengthen the commercial case significantly.

Probe on decision-making. She booked independently but no one else was mentioned. Ask directly: 'Is this something you would be in a position to move forward on yourself, or would others need to be involved?' Do this early to avoid a late-stage stall.

Quantify the time cost together during the meeting. She mentioned four days on one questionnaire, 326 questions on another, and a 150-page T&C document on a third. Work with her to estimate total hours lost per month — this will make the ROI conversation concrete and personal rather than theoretical.

Do not oversell automation. Karen is sophisticated and already understands the limitations of pure AI responses. She will be sceptical of anything that sounds like a generic AI tool. Lead with the human oversight and customisation angle, and use the AI tooling as a supporting capability rather than the headline.
